\begin{center}\begin{abstract}
		\noindent The Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990 placed limits on NO2 and SO2 emissions from coal fired power plants, which were significantly responsible for reducing the prevalence of acid rain in the US. Coal fired power plants partly met the emission requirements by demanding coal with a lower percentage of low sulfur. The change in demand for low sulfur coal reallocated coal mining away from some areas and towards other areas, creating an instrumental variable which we use to measure the downstream impact of coal mining on drinking water quality. An additional coal mine upstream of a community water system's water intake causes a large and statistically significant increase in SDWA violations from failing to test and submit drinking water quality reports to regulators. Regulators conducted more sanitary visits at CWSs that were exposed to more upstream coal mining but issued fewer formal enforcement actions to those systems. This pattern provides evidence that regulators applied discretion in allocating visits and punishment to CWSs and CWSs outsourced some water quality testing to regulators.

\end{abstract}\end{center}

\section{Institutional Background}
\subsection{The Safe Drinking Water Act}

The Safe Drinking Water Act regulates the quality of drinking water by codifying a list of contaminants that cannot be present in drinking water above contaminant specific maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) and assigning roles and responsibilities to CWSs, USEPA, and state EPA's to meet the requirements. The USEPA promulgates lists of regulated contaminants, their MCL's, best available technologies to remove them, and required treatment techniques for a subset of contaminants. The SDWA was amended twice between 1985 and 2005, and the EPA has promulgated 20 rule changes that expanded the list of regulated contaminants, changed the monitoring and reporting requirements, added treatment technique requirements. Contaminants can be grouped into inorganic chemicals, volatile organic chemicals, synthetic organic chemicals, radionuclides, and bacterials. The CWSs are responsible for supplying drinking water, removing contaminants, testing for contaminants, and reporting those tests to the regulator. Failure to meet their responsibilities leads to regulatory violations; a monitoring and reporting violation if they fail to monitor and submit water test results to regulators and an MCL violations if pollutants are found above regulatory levels in their tests. When states develop the administrative capacity and state laws to enforce and monitor CWS compliance with the SDWA they become the primary enforcers and regulators of the SDWA or SDWA primacy states. Primacy states receive and review test results from CWSs and are responsible for enforcing CWS monitoring schedules, issuing plans to bring violating CWSs into compliance, and issuing exemptions and variances to CWSs. Except for a subset of violating circumstances and mandatory notifications to consumers that CWSs must do when in violation, the SDWA gives primacy states wide discretionary power to bring CWSs into compliance and allows tailored plans for each CWS and violation. The SDWA forms the floor of water quality and states are able to pass stricter drinking water quality regulations.

\subsection{CWS Monitoring, Reporting, and Treating Drinking Water}

CWSs are in compliance with the SDWA on a continuous scale that depends on which contaminants are below the MCL, and which contaminant tests they have submitted to the regulator on time. A CWS incurs an MCL violation when a measured contaminant concentration in finished water exceeds the promulgated standard; an MCL violation is direct evidence that delivered water fails a health-based criterion. A Monitoring and Reporting (MR) violation is procedural: it arises when a system fails to collect required samples on schedule, collects fewer than the required number, or fails to submit results to its state primacy agency on time. Because SDWA relies on self-reporting, a system that does not monitor generates no data — it is not presumed in violation of the MCL, only of the monitoring requirement itself. A third category, Treatment Technique (TT) violations, arises under rules such as the Surface Water Treatment Rule that regulate process compliance rather than an effluent standard.

The SDWA MCL requirements for arsenic and nitrates remained the same between 1985 and 2005.\footnote{The other inorganic chemicals regulated between 1985 and 2005 began with barium, cadmium, chromium, lead, mercury, selenium, and silver, with the addition of antimony, asbestos, Beryllium, cyanide, nickel, and nitrite by 2005.} Once VOC and SOC MCL's were established their MCL's did not change between 1985 and 2005. A subset of SOCs were regulated contaminants across the time period from 1985 to 2005.\footnote{In 1985 SOC MCL's were established for contaminants 2,4-D, 2,4,5-TP (Silvex), endrin, lindane, methoxychlor, and toxaphene. Small groundwater systems with favorable sanitary survey history may be approved by the state for reduced monitoring of one sample per quarter. Inorganic chemicals (IOCs), VOCs, and SOCs, CWS using ground water only the testing required every three years testing and using surface water required yearly testing. In 1991 Phase II Rules (56 FR 3526, 1991) changed the testing and monitoring timeline to a nine year timeline cycle consisting of three three year cycles during which tests for IOCs, VOCs, and SOCs were required to be tested for four consecutive quarters to establish a baseline level by December 1995 and then once a year for surface water systems and once every three years for groundwater systems and these results had to be sent to the states. The second three-year compliance period ran from 1996–1998. The monitoring schedule for that period depended on what was detected in the first period (1993–1995): CWSs with no detections in 1993–1995 could reduce monitoring in the second (1996–1998) and third compliance period (1999–2001). The next nine year timeline began in 2002 with the first cycle ending in 2004 and the next cycle starting in 2005.

The EPA Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS) data accessed through the Enforcement and Compliance History Online (ECHO) system contains the universe of CWS violations. Each violation entry contains the date of the start and end of the violation, the type of violation, the contaminant, the severity, the agency responsible for enforcing the violation, and the penalty or compliance action the agency took to bring the CWS back into compliance.

Audits of violations of the SDWIS data during this time period note that violations are underreported (a survey of 1800 CWSs found that 10\% of monitoring and reporting violations were in the system but that 95\% of the monitoring and reporting violations in the system were accurately reported).

We measure the impact of coal production on drinking water quality by comparing the water quality from CWS's that source their water from watersheds with coal mines to CWS's that source their water from watersheds upstream of coal mines. This approach treats systems sourcing water upstream as counterfactuals to systems co-located with coal production. This follows the standard approach in the literature measuring the impact of policies or pollutants on water quality (\cite{keiser}; \cite{keiser};\cite{china cancer upstream}). The EPA collects data on the location of CWS water supply intakes. In order to protect the security of the CWS we are only able to observe the HUC12 which all water intakes belonging to a CWS are located. A HUC (hyrdrological unit code) is a 2-12 digit code assigned by the United States Geological Survey to each geographical area that drains into the same stream segment. Smaller geographic areas are represented by more HUC digits, for example the lower 48 US states are divided into 18 HUC2's and over 87000 HUC12's. For example a river will have tributaries such as creeks - a HUC12 is the area that drains into the creek (see image \ref{img:huc12}). HUC12 watershed boundary data is from the National Hydrography Dataset Plus data. HUC12's are the smallest geographical HU's available for the entire US. HUC12 watersheds are small enough geographic areas that coal mining activities which impact the hydrology surrounding the mine will impact the HUC12 water quality; the water quality of a CWS which sources water from that HUC12 may be affected too. 

It is possible for CWS's to obtain water from multiple HUC12's. We consider a CWS to be co-located with a coal mine if any of its primary water intakes are located in a HUC12 where coal mining took place. We consider a CWS to be located upstream of a coal mine if its primary water intakes are located in a HUC12 that is upstream of a HUC12 where coal mining takes places and none of its water intakes are co-located with a coal mine.

\subsection{Regulator Enforcement and Compliance}

The SDWA was directly enforced with direct agreements between states with primacy and the violating CWS. The SDWA gave primacy states the responsibility to regulate CWSs through their own compliance and enforcement strategies without prescribed specific actions. The states were required to receive CWSs test results, were empowered to conduct investigations and sanitary visits, enact enforcement in a timely manner, and given discretion over issuing CWSs variances and exemptions but were otherwise given discretion to create enforcement and compliance strategies. The SDWA gave regulators the legal authority to conduct sanitary visits and investigations of any CWS, but otherwise, states were empowered to devise their own enforcement techniques and create specific compliance plans as long as those plans were enacted quickly enough and brought the CWS back into compliance. Without a prescriptive enforcement legal strategy, in theory, each state could design a tailored compliance plan for a particular violation at a particular CWS. Each pollutant may require a different technology, and there may exist multiple technologies that have different costs or technical requirements that a CWS may or may not be able to maintain or pay for. 

The formal enforcement sequence begins with detection — either through system self-reporting or state inspection — followed by informal compliance assistance (letters, notices of violation) and, if violations persist, formal administrative actions. Under 42 USC §300g-3, when EPA determines that a state has not commenced enforcement within 30 days of notification of a violation, EPA must issue a federal administrative compliance order (ACO) or commence a civil action. In non-primacy states, EPA acts directly without the 30-day waiting period.

SDWA enforcement responsibility is primarily delegated to state primacy agencies; as of 2005, 55 of 57 states and territories held primacy for public water system supervision (Congressional Research Service, R46652). To obtain primacy, a state must adopt regulations at least as stringent as NPDWRs, maintain inspection and penalty authority, conduct sanitary surveys, and report violations to EPA.

Enforcement usually took the form of compliance plans requiring the CWS to directly address the cause of the violation and rarely included civil or crimial financial penalties. Compliance plans could still be costly to the CWS if they required installing treatment infrastructure or conducting tests. 

Empirical evidence indicates that formal penalties generate substantial general deterrence: Shimshack and Ward (2005) find that a single enforcement fine reduces the statewide violation rate by roughly two-thirds in the following year, primarily through spillover effects to uninspected systems. Administrative orders without financial penalties show no detectable compliance effect, suggesting that the monetary component is the operative deterrent (Shimshack and Ward 2005, 2008).

Civil penalties before 1996 were capped at \$5,000 per violation per day; the 1996 Amendments raised this to \$25,000 per day (adjusted for inflation in subsequent years). Administrative penalties below \$5,000 require notice and public hearing; those between \$5,000 and \$25,000 require a formal on-the-record hearing; penalties exceeding \$25,000 proceed to federal district court via DOJ referral. EPA referred on average approximately 420 cases per year to DOJ for civil or criminal judicial enforcement between 1998 and 2011; total civil judicial penalties averaged approximately \$93 million per year (Shimshack 2014, p. 344). Ordinary SDWA noncompliance does not trigger criminal liability; criminal provisions are reserved for tampering with a water system (up to 20 years imprisonment, 42 USC §300i-1) and willful underground injection control violations.

States and EPA also have several flexibility mechanisms that reduce enforcement burden: variances (available to systems whose raw water quality prevents MCL compliance despite best technology), exemptions (up to nine years for small systems serving 3,300 or fewer), and — after the 1996 Amendments — compliance schedules tied to consolidation or ownership transfer plans (42 USC §300g-3(h); Congressional Research Service, R46652).

The 1996 SDWA Amendments (P.L. 104-182, August 6, 1996) replaced the mandatory 25-contaminants-every-three-years schedule with a risk-based, cost-benefit framework (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, History of Drinking Water Regulations). Key changes relevant to monitoring and enforcement: the per-violation civil penalty ceiling rose from \$5,000/day to \$25,000/day; streamlined administrative order authority eliminated prior public-hearing requirements before EPA could issue orders; states were required to have administrative penalty authority as a condition of maintaining primacy; and the Standardized Monitoring Framework was supplemented by Alternative Monitoring Guidelines allowing states to modify groundwater monitoring frequencies for non-vulnerable systems (42 USC §300g-3; Congressional Research Service, R46652).

States were responsible for issuing variances and exemptions to CWS, which allowed a CWS to not comply with an MCL, MR, or TT requirement if the state determined that there was limited health effects of allowing the variance, and the risk of the contaminant was low. This power allowed the states to determine the scope of what SDWA requirements needed to be complied with. \subsection{The Acid Rain Program's Impact on Coal Production}

The Acid Rain Program (ARP) was signed into law on November 15, 1990, under the Acid Deposition Control (Title IV) of the Clean Air Act amendments. The purpose of Title IV was to reduce emissions of sulfur dioxide (SO2) and nitrogen oxides (NOx), the primary precursors of acid rain, from fossil fuel burning power plants.\footnote{Annual SO2 and NOx emissions goals were set to 10 million and 2 million fewer tons, respectively, from their 1980 emission levels \cite{lattanzio2022clean}}. 

The ARP used a cap and trade system to meet SO2 goals. In two phases the SO2 emissions from facilities built before November 15, 1990 were capped at a fraction of each facility's individual baseline SO2 emissions in 1985. In Phase I, the EPA and states with permitting capacity issued emission permits and allowances to 110 high polluting electricity generating facilities which used fossil fuels - mostly coal. The electric power industry accounted for 90\% of coal demand in 2000 (\cite{freme2000us}). The Phase I targetted facilities were required to achieve compliance with SO2 emission reductions by 1995. Compliance began even earlier because Phase I affected plants were required to apply to the EPA or state permitters for binding permits and compliance plans by 1993.\footnote{Title IV required the phase I facilities to submit an application for an emissions permit with a compliance plan within 27 months of the passage of the ARP (CAA Title IV. sec 408. c.1).} Facilities built after November 15, 1990 were not issued any SO2 allowances and any emissions they produced had to be purchased from facilities with allowances through an auction. Phase II capped the SO2 emissions from facilities with power generation capacity greater than 75 megawatts. The deadline for compliance with phase II was January 1, 2000. The ARP achieved 100\% compliance (\cite{lattanzio2022clean}). 

As opposed to SO2 emissions, the ARP did not limit total NOx emissions from sources; instead it limited the NOx emissions per mmBTU of fossil fuels burned.
NOx limits affected coal fired facilities at the same time they came under SO2 emission limits. For all other in two phases. Phase I NOx rules were promulgated in 1995. Phase II NOx regulation was promulagted in 2000.

Regulated facilities could meet the requirements by changing their fuel to ones that emit fewer SO2 and NOx, or installing abatement technology. The ARP incentivised coal fired power plants to switch to achieve emissions reduction from switching the type or quality of fossil fuel consumed - in practice this could be achieved through coal with less sulfur. It also subsidized a variety of coal firing technologies which reduced the emissions of SO2 and NOx. Title IV required electricity producers who sought to meet the emissions requirements by shutting down power generating units to make up electricity production that it lost 408 c.1.B. Title IV allowed regulated  facilities to exceed emission requirements during emergencies and guaranteeing facilities would not be shut-down even if they fail to obtain an emissions permit or compliance plan.

The ARP increased the demand for low sulfur coal and reduced the demand for high sulfur coal (\cite{epaarpcoalemp}; \cite{stratford2015coalprod}). We summarize the trends in coal output at the HUC12 level using HUC12. We match coal mine locations from the Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) to HUC12 boundaries from USGS; we match coal mine production data to each coal mine using data from the Energy Information Administration; finally we measure the sulfur content of the coal bed within a HUC12 using coal bed characterisitics from the Coal Quality (COALQUAL) database from USGS. The COALQUAL database consists of over 7000 coal bed samples which we match to HUC12 watershed boundary maps from the USGS National Hydrography Dataset Plus data. The HUC12 coal bed characterisitics are the average characterisitics of all samples within 40 km's of the HUC12 boundary. We aggregate individual coal mine data to the HUC12 level.

\section{Instrumenting for Coal Production with Coal Sulfur and The ARP}

The locations of CWS water intakes and coal mines are not randomly assigned throughout the landscape. Coal mines will site where they are able to pass environmental review, and a CWS downstream of a coal mine will increase the chances of failing an environmental review. The OLS estimate of the impact of coal mines on drinking water quality violations will suffer from omitted variable bias from unmeasured variables that influence both where a mine sites and how capable a CWS is at managing violations. For example a CWS with political power may be able to influence regulators to prevent polluting industries from siting upstream, those systems are more capable at avoiding violations.

The omitted variable bias is not likely. The number of coal mines is an object that arises from the equilibration of national coal demand and supply coal and the number of violations at a CWS will not impact the equilibrium of the national coal market.

Sulfur is endogenous to water quality; higher sulfur levels are associated with more sulfide minerals in the rock that oxidize and cause AMD. High sulfide rocks in the geology of a watershed naturally oxidize and leach inorganic chemicals into the water, raising the baseline contaminant level, and those rocks are more likely to contain coal seams that attract coal mining. Unobserved CWSs with greater political power and resources will be able to site their water intakes in areas with cleaner baseline ambient water quality. Geology can be partly controlled for in a regression by including CWS fixed effects.

The Acid Rain Program created variation in coal production that depended on the sulfur content of the coal after 1995. The ARP induced coal powered electricity plants to demand coal with a low percentage of sulfur in order to meet the SO2 and NOx requirements of the Acid Rain Program. Coal supply from low sulfur coal beds increased relative to coal supply from high sulfur coal beds. The number of coal mines a CWS was downstream of depended on the sulfur content of the coal in the upstream watershed after 1995. The likelihood of a coal mine opening in a low sulfur coal watershed upstream of CWS increased independently of the characteristics of the CWS after 1995. The ARP and the timing of the first phase were both exogenous to CWS drinking water quality and CWS characteristics. The ARP was a national clean air policy that did not consider its impact on water quality. The timing of the ARP did not coincide with major drinking water regulations. 

$UpstreamCoalSulfur\%\times Post95$ is an instrument that predicts the number of coal mines upstream of a CWS while being independent of the omitted variables that determine drinking water quality violations. Local CWS characteristics regarding water quality or local power over source protection did not affect the scope of the ARP, nor how stringent the ARP law was, nor the degree to which low sulfur coal should be part of the abatement technology, nor the date of the phase I of the ARP; thus, $Cov(\text{political power of CWS}, Coal Sulfur \% \times Post95)=0$. Assuming that the relationship between CWS characteristics important for water quality and the upstream sulfur content did not change after 1995 then $f(CoalSulfur_c \times Post95_t, \text{CWS characteristic})=f(CoalSulfur_c \times Post95_t)f(\text{CWS characteristic})$. The instrument would fail independence if the unobserved CWS characteristics that affect drinking water quality violations changed after 1995 according to the amount of upstream coal sulfur percentage.

Exclusion and independence of instrument: sulfur x Acid Rain Program
The omitted variable is a local PWS or community's ability to influence where mines are sited. The instrument (sulfur X acid rain program) meets the exclusion restriction (Cov(political power, sulfur X acid rain program=0) because the legislation targeting national air quality and primarily envisioned SO2 scrubbers rather than low sulfur coal as the abatement solution. Local political power over water quality did not affect how stringent the national ARP law was, nor the degree that low sulfur coal should be a part of the abatement technology. The instrument is independent because by knowing about sulfur X ARP tells us nothing about how much political power a local PWS has about mine siting. Likewise, ARP was a national air quality regulation and not influenced by local water and the importance of sulfur percentage was not the primary abatement mechanism forseen in the legislation. Sulfur is endogenous to water quality: higher sulfur levels are associated with more sulfide minerals in the rock that oxidize and cause AMD. Post95 is exogenous to water quality since ARP was not passed to address water quality. Exclusion comes from the $sulfur*post95$ and controlling for observable PWS characteristics and geochemistry at water intakes.

\section{Assessing monotonicity}

Why the Economic Mechanism Supports Monotonicity
The compliance channel is a regulatory cost shifter on the supply side:

ARP Phase I required SO2 reductions, primarily from high-sulfur coal combustion
Utilities substituted toward low-sulfur coal and away from high-sulfur coal
Demand for high-sulfur coal fell everywhere post-1995, proportional to sulfur content

This is a market-wide demand shift — not a behavioral response that depends on unobserved unit characteristics. There is no natural story for a defier HUC: a region with higher sulfur content that expanded production post-ARP because of the regulation. Defiance would require that the same mine with high-sulfur would have increased production after ARP but would reduce its production post ARP if it had low-sulfur. A high-sulfur mine that maintains production because its customer is an electricity generator that installs scrubbers is not a defier — it is a never-taker (the instrument failed to shift its treatment). As long as scrubber adoption is not positively correlated with sulfur in a way that causes production to increase post-ARP in high-sulfur areas, this is not a violation of monotonicity, just reduced first-stage power. To be a defier with scrubbers requires a power plant to charge less for power to customers after installing scrubbers, it is not profitable to do so because scrubbers are expensive to install and increase operating costs the profit maximizing response is to raise electricity costs which result in a fall in demand for power and for coal. Scrubbers are expensive to install which is the reason fuel switching was the primary compliance method at power plants.

High-sulfur power plants may have bought more permits rather than cutting production. This could create defiers if those high-sulfur power plants increased their electricity output and demand for high sulfur coal causing an increase in coal supply to meet that demand.

The two stage least squares regression estimates that an additional coal mine upstream of a community water system's water intake leads to significantly more nitrate and arsenic violations, an additional 20 and 14.6 days out of the year respectively. The first stage F statistic is 27.5. The OLS regressions use the number of coal mines active during the year in the HUC12 upstream of a CWS's intake as the main explanatory variable and use CWS and year fixed effects. The OLS results underestimate the effect of coal mines on violations, in general the OLS impact of coal mines is five times smaller than the two stage least squares regression coefficient, suggesting that the omitted variable bias is negative. 

Coal production fell most acutely in high sulfur areas after 1995, and a positive relationship between coal production and coal related violations predicts that coal related violations would fall with increasing upstream sulfur after 1995 relative to before 1995. The reduced form effect of the instrument measures how much more (or less) a one-percentage-point increase in upstream sulfur affects violations after ARP Phase I relative to before. An additional percent of coal sulfur upstream leads to statistically significantly fewer days a CWS experiences a nitrate, arsenic, or inorganic chemical violation of any kind after 1995 compared to before 1995. This suggests that in areas where coal mining is more likely to close the number of days in violation falls.

The entire increase in violations from upstream mining is driven by monitoring and reporting violations rather than maximum contaminant violations. The regressions presented in table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_minevio_mr} limit the dependent variables to monitoring and reporting violations for inorganic chemical and radionuclide pollution. An additional mine upstream of a CWS leads to 19, 14.5, and 14 more days of monitoring and reporting violations for nitrates, arsenic, and general inorganic chemical pollution respectively, comprising the entire effect observed when the dependent variable combines monitoring and reporting violations with maximum contamination level violations. Inorganic chemical violations are statistically significant at the 10\% level when violations are limited to MR violations and either MCL or MR violations. The impact of coal mining on downstream CWS maximum contaminant level violations for nitrates, arsenic, inorganic chemicals, and radionuclides is presented in table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_minevio_mcl}. The two stage least squares regression of the effect of the number of coal mines upstream leads to a statistically and economically insignificant change in the amount of time a CWS spends in an arsenic and nitrate violation, the effect is less than an additional day out of the year. An additional coal mine upstream is associated with two fewer days of maximum contaminant level violations for inorganic chemicals and radionuclides according to the two stage least squares regressions; however, the effect is not statistically significant at any level.

Upstream coal mining does not have a statistically significant effect on violations from pollution that is not typically associated with coal mining. Table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_nonminevio_allcat} presents the results of regressions that use a dependent variable combining monitoring and reporting and maximum contaminant level violations for total coliforms and VOCs. \section{Strategic Violation Behavior}

Adapting a static model of a regulator and a CWS from \cite{kang2021understanding}, which is an adaptation of the one developed by \cite{mookherjee1994marginal}, leads to comparative statics that demonstrate that an increase in water pollution results in an increase in monitoring and reporting violations. CWSs choose a negligence level $a \in [0,\bar{a}]$ which measures how much they avoid the requirements of drinking water quality regulations, from maximum negligence $\bar{a}$ to full compliance 0. Failing to conduct or submit tests to the regulator is an example of CWS negligence. Regulators do not directly observe CWSs choice of $a$. CWSs face a compliance cost, $\theta$, that is a random variable drawn from $\Theta$ which is a strictly increasing and continuously differentiable function $F(\cdot)$ with support $(0,\bar{\theta})$. CWSs know their draw of $\theta$, but regulators only know the distribution of $\Theta$. A compliance cost is determined by both a CWS's ability to provide drinking and manage a system, for example the skills of managerial and technical staff, and by the costs the CWS spends to treat the water, for example the cost of filtration equipment or sterilization chemicals. assume compliance costs depend explicitly on the amount of upstream coal mining, such that $\theta (m)$ is continuously differentiable and non-decreasing in m.

CWSs receive a benefit $\theta b(a)$ from having compliance costs $\theta$ and choosing a negligence level $a$. A CWS's benefit from not conducting a test for a specific contaminant are the labor and lab fees for sampling they did not have to spend. The opportunity cost to the CWS of choosing a compliance level $a$ relative to maximum negligence $\bar{a}$ is $\theta [b(\bar{a})-b(a)]$.

The number of violations a CWS incurs is a random variable $K$ that follows a Poisson distribution with mean $a$. Given negligence level $a$, observed violations follow a Poisson process:

\[Pr(K = k | a) = e^{-a}  \frac{a^k}{k!}\]

The penalty schedule the CWS faces for incurring $k$ violations is given by $\epsilon(k)$ which is non-decreasing and continuously differentiable. The penalty schedule is a function that includes the likelihood of the regulator enforcing the penalty at each violation and the cost of the violation. The regulator can only base the penalty on $k$ because it cannot observe negligence or cost of compliance. Assuming the CWS is risk neutral the expected penalty for a risk neutral CWS is 
\begin{equation}
    e(a)\equiv exp(-a)\sum^\infty_{k=0}\frac{\epsilon(k)}{k!}a^k.
\end{equation}

The expected payoff to the CWS from negligence level $a$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cws_benefitcost}
    -\theta(m)[b(\bar{a})-b(a)]-e(a).
\end{equation}

A negligence schedule, $a(\cdot)$, is implemented by a penalty schedule, $e(\cdot)$, if $a(\theta)$ maximizes equation \ref{eq:cws_benefitcost} for all $\theta \in \Theta$. Combining the first order condition with the $a(\cdot)$ implemented by $e(\cdot)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{cws_foc}
    \theta b'[a(\theta)]=e'[a(\theta)]
\end{equation}
whenever $a(\theta)>0$.

Proposition 1: CWSs choose higher negligence levels as compliance costs increase, $\partial a/ \partial \theta>0$.

Assuming $b(a)$ is a strictly increasing function and the F.O.C. in equation \ref{cws_foc} solves the CWS maximization,
\[\frac{\partial a}{\partial \theta}=-\frac{b'(a)}{\theta b''(a)-e''(a)}>0\]

CWS's with higher compliance costs, for example, those with more expensive to run filtration machinery, find the marginal benefit of an additional unit of negligence $\theta·b'(a)$ larger relative to the marginal expected penalty $e'(a)$. They optimally reduce testing and compliance and increase the number of monitoring and reporting violations.

Proposition 2: More upstream mining raises negligence, $\partial a/\partial m >0$.
\[\frac{\partial a}{\partial m}=-\frac{\theta'(m)b'(a)}{\theta(m) b''(a)-e''(a)}>0\]

When mining raises pollution levels which increases the costs of water treatment and the compliance costs, the optimal negligence level increases, reducing testing and reporting and increasing the number of monitoring and reporting violations.

The measured increase in monitoring and reporting violations caused by increased coal mining upstream are results in the same direction as the predicted direction of the comparative static results. 

ARP Phase I (post-1995) differentially reduced production at high-sulfur upstream mines, shifting the contamination burden facing downstream CWSs. CWSs facing higher contamination strategically substitute MR violations for MCL violations — skipping required monitoring to avoid detecting maximum-contaminant-level breaches. The increase in negligence and monitoring and reporting violations following an increase in coal mining in lower sulfur areas due to the Acid Rain Program could have been offset by regulator by increasing enforcement of CWS violations.

Proposition 3: Higher enforcement rates for violations increase compliance and reduce the number of monitoring and reporting violations. Assume higher enforcement rates at every $k$ increase the penalty schedule $\epsilon(k)$ by a fixed amount $\lambda>1$, such that $\lambda\epsilon(k)$, then $\partial a/\partial \lambda<0$.

\[\frac{\partial a}{\partial \lambda}=\frac{e'(a)}{\theta b''(a)-\lambda e''(a)}<0\]

\section{Empirical Structural Estimation: Counterfactual raising probability of enforcement}

We use a dynamic discrete choice model where each year a CWS chooses the action to comply or MR-violate, where MR carries an implicit per-period cost $k_{MR}$ (the value of concealment) and shifts the next-period probability of being placed under enforcement by Δπ(s) ≡ Pr(enf' | s, MR) − Pr(enf' | s, comply). Using the Hotz–Miller CCP estimator we recover k_MR from the data. The counterfactual then asks: holding the structural primitives — including k_MR — fixed, by how much would the regulator have to widen the action-conditional enforcement gap Δπ(s) (specifically, raise p_01^m, the probability of enforcement following an MR violation) to drive aggregate MR violation rates to half their observed level? To zero?

Each year a CWS chooses to either comply, which carries labor and administrative costs of conducting tests and reporting them, or MR violate, which carries a risk of enforcement and avoids the costs of tests and reporting. The choice also changes the probability that the CWS faces an enforcement action next year. To recover the implicit cost the CWS attaches to an MR violation, $k_{MR}$, we need to disentangle "what they pay today" from "what they expect to pay tomorrow."

\section{A Dynamic Model of CWS Compliance}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Environment}

Time is discrete and indexed by $t = 1, 2, \ldots$. A community water system
(CWS) $i$ chooses an action $a_{it} \in \{0, 1\}$ each period, where $a_{it} = 1$
denotes \emph{compliance} (conducting tests and reporting them) and $a_{it} = 0$
denotes \emph{negligence}, which produces a monitoring-and-reporting (MR)
violation. Compliance carries a per-period administrative cost $k_{\text{comply}}$;
negligence avoids this cost but raises the probability of future enforcement and
carries an implicit reputational/operational cost $k_{\text{MR}}$.

\paragraph{State space.} The CWS faces a state $s_{it} = (x_{it}, \varepsilon_{it})$
with two components:
\begin{itemize}
\item $x_{it} \in \mathcal{X}$ is the \emph{observed} state, including
      enforcement status ($0 = $ no active enforcement, $1 = $ currently under
      enforcement) and a discretized mining-exposure measure. $x_{it}$ is
      observed by both the CWS and the econometrician.
\item $\varepsilon_{it} = \big(\varepsilon_{it}(0), \varepsilon_{it}(1)\big)
      \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is a vector of action-specific payoff shocks. The CWS
      observes $\varepsilon_{it}$ before choosing $a_{it}$; the econometrician
      never observes it. These shocks capture transitory variation in the
      relative attractiveness of complying versus violating (e.g., temporary
      staffing constraints, lab availability, managerial attention).
\end{itemize}

Per-period payoff is additively separable in $\varepsilon$:
\begin{equation}
U(a, x_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}(a),
\label{eq:flow}
\end{equation}
where $U(1, x) = -k_{\text{comply}} - c(x)$ and $U(0, x) = -k_{\text{MR}} - c(x)$,
and $c(x)$ is the direct cost of being in enforcement state $x$ (normalized so
that $c(0) = 0$ and $c(1) = 1$).\footnote{Because $c(x)$ depends only on the
enforcement state and not on the action, it cancels from the flow-utility
difference: $U(0, x) - U(1, x) = k_{\text{comply}} - k_{\text{MR}}$,
independent of $x$. The enforcement cost $c(x)$ enters the estimating equation
only through the continuation value $\bar V(x)$ in \eqref{eq:Vbar}, and only
through the difference $\bar V(1) - \bar V(0)$.}

\paragraph{Distributional and independence assumptions.} Following
\citet{rust1987optimal} and \citet{hotz1993conditional}, we impose:
\begin{description}
\item[(A1) Additive separability.] Payoffs take the form in
      \eqref{eq:flow}.
\item[(A2) T1EV shocks.] The $\varepsilon_{it}(a)$ are i.i.d.\ across $i$,
      $t$, and $a$, drawn from a Type-1 Extreme Value distribution. The scale
      is normalized to one ($\sigma_\varepsilon \equiv 1$); together with
      $c(1) = 1$ this fixes the model's units, so $k_{\text{MR}}$ is identified
      in units of the enforcement-state cost $c(1)$ rather than dollars.
\item[(A3) Conditional independence.] The transition density factors as
      $f(x', \varepsilon' \mid x, \varepsilon, a) = f_x(x' \mid x, a)
      \cdot f_\varepsilon(\varepsilon')$. That is, conditional on the current
      observed state and action, next period's observed state $x'$ does not
      depend on the current $\varepsilon$, and next period's $\varepsilon'$ is
      drawn independently of the past.
\end{description}
Assumption (A3) is the Conditional Independence Assumption of
\citet{rust1987optimal}. It is what makes $\varepsilon$ a transitory taste
shock rather than a persistent unobservable, and is essential for the
representation derived below.

\subsection{The CWS's dynamic programming problem}

The CWS chooses a policy $\{a_{it}\}$ to maximize the expected
discounted sum of payoffs, $\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \beta^{j}
\big[ U(a_{i,t+j}, x_{i,t+j}) + \varepsilon_{i,t+j}(a_{i,t+j}) \big]$,
with discount factor $\beta \in (0, 1)$ (set to $\beta = 0.95$ in
estimation). By the principle of optimality, the value function over the
\emph{full} state satisfies the Bellman equation
\begin{equation}
V(x, \varepsilon) = \max_{a \in \{0, 1\}} \Big\{
U(a, x) + \varepsilon(a) + \beta \, \mathbb{E}\!\left[ V(x', \varepsilon')
\,\big|\, x, a \right] \Big\}.
\label{eq:bellman_full}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Integrating out the private shock}

Equation~\eqref{eq:bellman_full} is intractable as written because $V$ depends on
the unobserved $\varepsilon$. Under (A3), however, we can integrate
$\varepsilon'$ out of the continuation value. Define the choice-specific value
function
\begin{equation}
\bar v(a, x) \;\equiv\; U(a, x) + \beta \sum_{x'} \bar V(x') \, P(x' \mid x, a),
\label{eq:vbar}
\end{equation}
where $P(x' \mid x, a) \equiv f_x(x' \mid x, a)$ is the action-conditional
transition probability between observed states, and $\bar V(x)$ is the integrated value function
\begin{equation}
\bar V(x) \;\equiv\; \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon \!\left[ \max_{a}
\big\{ \bar v(a, x) + \varepsilon(a) \big\} \right].
\label{eq:Vbar}
\end{equation}
Under (A2), equation \eqref{eq:Vbar} has a closed form \cite{train2009discrete}:
\begin{equation}
\bar V(x) = \log \!\sum_{a \in \{0,1\}} \exp\!\left( \bar v(a, x) \right) + C,
\label{eq:logsumexp}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is a constant that is the same for every $x$ and therefore cancels in every
difference $\bar V(x) - \bar V(x')$ taken below.
Equations~\eqref{eq:vbar}--\eqref{eq:logsumexp} together constitute a fixed
point in $\bar v$ and $\bar V$ that depends only on the observed state $x$.

\subsection{The Hotz--Miller representation}

Given the choice-specific values $\bar v(a, x)$, the optimal action solves a
static problem with deterministic payoffs and T1EV shocks:
\begin{equation*}
a^*(x, \varepsilon) = \arg\max_{a \in \{0,1\}}
\big\{ \bar v(a, x) + \varepsilon(a) \big\}.
\end{equation*}
Standard logit algebra \cite[Ch.~3]{train2009discrete} yields the conditional
choice probability
\begin{equation}
\Pr(a \mid x) = \frac{\exp\!\left( \bar v(a, x) \right)}
{\sum_{a \in \{0, 1\}} \exp\!\left( \bar v(a, x) \right)}.
\label{eq:ccp}
\end{equation}
Taking log-odds between negligence ($a = 0 = MR$) and compliance ($a = 1 = comply$):
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\log \!\left[ \frac{\Pr(\text{MR} \mid x)}{\Pr(\text{comply} \mid x)} \right]
\;=\; \bar v(0, x) - \bar v(1, x)
\;\equiv\; \Delta \bar v(x).
\;}
\label{eq:logodds}
\end{equation}
Expanding the right hand side of equation \ref{eq:logodds}
\begin{equation}
\Delta \bar v(x) = \underbrace{\big[ U(0, x) - U(1, x) \big]}_{\text{flow utility difference}}
+ \beta \underbrace{\sum_{x'} \bar V(x')
\big[ P(x' \mid x, 0) - P(x' \mid x, 1) \big]}_{\text{continuation value difference}}.
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}

The flow utility difference 
is \[U(0, x) - U(1, x) = -k_{\text{MR}} - c(x) + k_{\text{comply}} + c(x) = k_{\text{comply}}-k_{\text{MR}}.\]

Expanding the summation of the continuation value difference over $x'\in(0,1)$ gives
\[\beta\left[\bar V(1)[P(1\mid x,0)-P(1\mid x,1)]+\bar V(0)[P(0\mid x,0)-P(0\mid x,1)]\right].\]
Since $P$ is a transition matrix the sum of probabilities for all states given an action $a$ sum to 1
\[P(0\mid x,0)=1-P(1\mid x,0)\]
\[P(0\mid x,1)=1-P(1\mid x,1).\]

After substituting the above,
\[\beta\left[\bar V(1)[P(1\mid x,0)-P(1\mid x,1)]+\bar V(0)[P(1\mid x,1)-P(1\mid x,0)]\right]\] can be written as 
\[\beta\left[(\bar V(1)-\bar V(0))[P(1\mid x,0)-P(1\mid x,1)]\right].\]
Let $\Delta\pi(x) \equiv P(1 \mid x,0) - P(1 \mid x, 1)$ be the change in the probability of transitioning to the enforcement state caused by MR-violating rather than complying. The continuation value is
\[\beta \Delta\pi(x) (\bar V(1)-\bar V(0)).\]
The full expression of equation \ref{eq:logodds}
\begin{equation}
    \;
\log \!\left[ \frac{\Pr(\text{MR} \mid x)}{\Pr(\text{comply} \mid x)} \right]
\;=\; k_{\text{comply}}-k_{\text{MR}}+\beta [\bar V(1)-\bar V(0)]\Delta\pi(x).
\label{eq:finalest}
\end{equation}

$\bar V$ can be expressed as a closed-form linear function using the \cite{arcidiaconomiller2011} inversion. Combining the CCP formula \eqref{eq:ccp} with the log-sum-exp expression
\eqref{eq:logsumexp} and taking logs yields the identity
\begin{equation}
\bar V(x) = \bar v(a, x) - \log \hat\Pr(a \mid x) + C,
\qquad \text{for any } a \in \{0, 1\}.
\label{eq:am_inversion}
\end{equation}

Applying
\eqref{eq:am_inversion} to both states and differencing:
\begin{equation}
\bar V(1) - \bar V(0) = \big[\bar v(1, 1) - \bar v(1, 0)\big]
- \big[\log \hat\Pr(1 \mid 1) - \log \hat\Pr(1 \mid 0)\big].
\label{eq:vbar_diff}
\end{equation}

$\bar v(1, 0)$ and $\bar v(1, 1)$ can be solved for in 2x2 system of equations. Choosing the comply action ($a = 1$) as the reference and substituting \eqref{eq:am_inversion} into the Bellman \eqref{eq:vbar} for $a = 1$ gives a linear system in $\bar v(1, \cdot)$ alone:
\begin{equation}
\bar v(1, x) = U(1, x) + \beta \sum_{x'} P(x' \mid x, 1)
\big[ \bar v(1, x') - \log \hat\Pr(1 \mid x') \big]
+ \text{const}.
\label{eq:vbar1_system}
\end{equation}
Stacking over the two enforcement states $x \in \{0, 1\}$, let $\boldsymbol{\bar v}_1 = (\bar v(1, 0), \bar v(1, 1))'$, $\boldsymbol{U}_1 = (U(1, 0), U(1, 1))'$, $P_1$ the $2 \times 2$ comply-action transition matrix, and $\boldsymbol{\ell}_1 = (\log \hat\Pr(1 \mid 0), \log \hat\Pr(1 \mid 1))'$. Then
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\bar v}_1 = (I - \beta P_1)^{-1}
\left[ \boldsymbol{U}_1 - \beta P_1 \boldsymbol{\ell}_1 \right]
+ \text{const} \cdot \mathbf{1},
\label{eq:vbar1_solve}
\end{equation}
Equation \ref{eq:vbar1_solve} is closed-form and the right hand side can be calculated from the data. After solving $\boldsymbol{\bar v}_1$, the two entries of $\boldsymbol{\bar v}_1 = (\bar v(1, 0), \bar v(1, 1))'$ can be plugged into equation \ref{eq:vbar_diff}.

Starting from \eqref{eq:vbar1_solve}, substitute
$\boldsymbol{U}_1 = -k_{\text{comply}}\cdot\mathbf{1} - \boldsymbol{c}$:
%
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol{\bar v}_1
  &= (I-\beta P_1)^{-1}
     \!\left[-k_{\text{comply}}\mathbf{1} - \boldsymbol{c}
             - \beta P_1\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\right]
     + \text{const}\cdot\mathbf{1}
\label{eq:vbar1_expand}\\[4pt]
%
  &= -k_{\text{comply}}\,(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}\mathbf{1}
     -(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}
     -\beta\,(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}P_1\boldsymbol{\ell}_1
     + \text{const}\cdot\mathbf{1}.
\label{eq:vbar1_decomp}
\end{align}
%
Because $P_1$ is row-stochastic, $P_1\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}$, so
$(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}\mathbf{1} = (1-\beta)^{-1}\mathbf{1}$. Hence
$-k_{\text{comply}}(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}\mathbf{1}
= -\tfrac{k_{\text{comply}}}{1-\beta}\mathbf{1}$,
a scalar multiple of the ones vector. Taking the difference between any two elements of $\bar v_1$, it is a 2x1 vector, results in $-\tfrac{k_{\text{comply}}}{1-\beta}\mathbf{1}$ cancelling. Let $\Delta_x$ refer to the difference between the entry in any vector referring to action $a=1$ and the action $a=0$.

%
\begin{align}
\bar v(1,1)-\bar v(1,0)
  &= -\Delta_x\!\big[(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}\big]
     -\beta\,\Delta_x\!\big[(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}P_1\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\big],
\label{eq:vbar_diff_cv}
\end{align}
%
Substituting \eqref{eq:vbar_diff_cv} into \eqref{eq:vbar_diff} and
noting that the log-CCP difference is $\Delta_x\boldsymbol{\ell}_1
\equiv \log\hat{\Pr}(1\mid 1)-\log\hat{\Pr}(1\mid 0)$:
%
\begin{align}
\bar V(1)-\bar V(0)
  &= \underbrace{\big[\bar v(1,1)-\bar v(1,0)\big]}_{\eqref{eq:vbar_diff_cv}}
     -\underbrace{\big[\log\hat{\Pr}(1\mid 1)-\log\hat{\Pr}(1\mid 0)\big]}_{\Delta_x\boldsymbol{\ell}_1}
\nonumber\\[6pt]
  &= -\Delta_x\!\big[(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}\big]
     -\beta\,\Delta_x\!\big[(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}P_1\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\big]
     -\Delta_x\boldsymbol{\ell}_1,
\end{align}
%
After substitution, the expanded expression that can be taken to the data and estimated by OLS is
\begin{equation}
    \;
\log \!\left[ \frac{\Pr(\text{MR} \mid x)}{\Pr(\text{comply} \mid x)} \right]
\;=\; (k_{\text{comply}}-k_{\text{MR}})+\beta \left[-\Delta_x\!\big[(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}\big]
     -\beta\,\Delta_x\!\big[(I-\beta P_1)^{-1}P_1\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\big]
     -\Delta_x\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\right]\Delta\pi(x).
\label{eq:finalest}
\end{equation}

\section{Empirical Structural Estimation: Counterfactual raising probability of enforcement}

Using the Hotz–Miller CCP estimator we recover k_MR from the data. The counterfactual then asks: holding the structural primitives — including k_MR — fixed, by how much would the regulator have to widen the action-conditional enforcement gap Δπ(s) (specifically, raise p_01^m, the probability of enforcement following an MR violation) to drive aggregate MR violation rates to half their observed level? To zero?

Each year a CWS picks comply, which carries labor and administrative cost of conducting tests and reporting them, or MR violate, which carries a risk of enforcement. The choice also changes the probability the CWS faces an enforcement action next year. To recover $k_{MR}$ — the implicit cost the CWS attaches to an MR violation — we need to disentangle "what they pay today" from "what they expect to pay tomorrow."

Each CWS-year is in one of two enforcement states:
- `no_enf`: no active enforcement action
- `enf`: currently under enforcement

### Section 3 — Structural Estimation (2-state regulatory response function, binary CCP)

**Step 1: 2-state regulatory response function (action-conditional transitions)**

State space: {`no_enf`, `enf`}. Transitions are **not exogenous** — SDWA enforcement is triggered by violations, so the regulator's transition matrix is action-conditional: P(s' | s, a) for a ∈ {comply, MR}. Estimate two 2×2 matrices by counting transitions separately within comply-years and MR-years across the 268 PWSIDs with enforcement records. Forward-simulate V₀(s) for s ∈ {no_enf, enf} under β = 0.95 using the optimal-policy-implied transition matrix. The action-conditional structure is what makes Δπ(s) ≡ Pr(enf' | s, MR) − Pr(enf' | s, comply) nonzero in Step 2 and is the source of dynamic deterrence in the model. Allow transitions to vary by enforcement intensity quartile.

Terminology: refer to this as a "regulatory response function" rather than a "regulatory machine" to avoid implying exogeneity. The Duflo et al. (2018) "machine" framing is inappropriate here because their inspection process was randomized; ours is endogenous to firm behavior.

**Step 2: CCP identification of k_MR**

\subsection{Mine opening and closing DiD}
We measure the impact of mine opening and closing on violations with the heterogenous robust estimator proposed by \cite{de2024difference} because mine openings and closing are different from each other and the design is complicated because the same pws experiences mines open and close and more than one mine opening.

We cannot do a TWFE to test the effect of closing and opening a mine on violations. The effect of closing and opening a mine will be biased since the effect of a mine opening and a mine closing are heterogeneous treatment effects \cite{de2020two}; \cite{de2023two}.

The heterogeneous estimator produces event study estimates that are weighted averages of actual-versus-status quo effects of the treated unit at time $l$ after the treated path leading to time $l$. The status quo effect is the outcome of a unit at time $l$ if the unit received in period one had remained every period, including period $l$. Uses switcher instead of treated.

Placebos are used to test the parallel trends and no anticipation assumption. It compares the outcome evolution of switchers to control groups for each period before switching to the period before switching and does a joint test on the equality of the placebo effects.

Interprettable as the effect of being exposed to a weakly larger number of coal mines.

We measure the sulfur characterisitics of a coal bed within a HUC12 watershed. The sulfur characteristics of coal beds in HUC12 come from the USGS COALQUAL database. Each observation in COALQUAL contains sample level results of chemical analyzes conducted on the sample, including sulfur percentage, sampling depth, and the geographic coordinates of the sample with latitude and longitude. The HUC12 watershed coal bed sulfur percentage is an average of the coal sulfur percentage of all COALQUAL samples collected within at most 50 kilometers from the boundary of the HUC12. Every mine located in a particular HUC12 watershed is assumed to extract coal with the sulfur percent of the HUC12 watershed coal bed sulfur percent. We investigate the impact of opening or closing a mine in a HUC12 by comparing violation rates at PWSs in HUC12's where a mine closed or opened to violation rates at a PWS upstream. Mine openings and closings are analyzed separately. A PWS is exposed to a mine opening if a mine opened in its intake HUC12 and none closed for five years following the opening. Similarly, a PWS is exposed to a mine closure if a mine closes in its intake HUC12 and none opened for five years following the closure. 
The event study equation for a mine opening is,
\begin{equation}\label{mineopen}
    Violation_{pt}=\sum_{j\in\{-5,...,5\}} \beta_j MineOpen_{t-j}+\tau_t+\rho_p+\epsilon_{pt}
\end{equation}
Where $Violation_{pt}$ is a binary indicator equal to one if the PWS $p$ experienced a specific type of SDWA violation in year $t$. $MineOpen_{t-j}$ is a binary variable equal to one if a mine opening occurred $j$ periods from time $t$. $\beta_j$ is the effect of being $j$ years from a mine opening. For mine closing analysis, we replace $MineOpen_{t-j}$ with $MineClose_{t-j}$.
