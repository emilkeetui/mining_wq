\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This note sets out and executes a measurement exercise: count the number of
people whose drinking water comes from a community water system (CWS) located
downstream of an active coal mine, track how that count evolved from 1990 to
the present, and validate the reconstruction against held-out data. The
central obstacle is that system-level population data do not exist in usable
form before the mid-2000s. We therefore \emph{reconstruct} pre-2005 population
using a transparent ratio-scaling procedure that combines (i) the boundaries
of each system's service area, (ii) decennial Census population counts inside
those boundaries, and (iii) the years when a system's reported population
\emph{is} observed, used to calibrate the reconstruction. Applied to the 367
community water systems ever one HUC12 directly downstream of an active coal
mine, the procedure recovers a complete 1990--2024 population series for 366
of them (99.7\%). Two validation exercises --- leave-one-anchor-out and
leave-one-decade-out --- discipline the two layers of the estimator
independently and show that aggregate exposure totals are accurate even where
individual systems are noisy. The document is written for readers who know
applied microeconomics but not the institutional details of U.S. drinking
water or the ``backcasting'' literature; both are introduced from scratch.
\end{abstract}

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:questions}

Let a \emph{community water system} (CWS) be ``downstream of coal'' in year
$t$ if at least one active coal mine lies upstream of the system's water
intake in the hydrological network (Section~\ref{sec:background} makes this
precise). Write $\mathcal{D}_t$ for the set of such systems in year $t$, and
let $S_{i,t}$ be the number of people served by system $i$ in year $t$. The
exercise answers four questions.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em,style=nextline]
  \item[Q1. Pre-period change (1990--2005).] How did total exposed population
    $\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_t}S_{i,t}$ change between 1990 and 2005? This is
    the period for which $S_{i,t}$ must be reconstructed. \textbf{Answered
    below, Section~\ref{sec:results}.}
  \item[Q2. Recent change (2005--present).] How did the same total change
    between 2005 and the present, where system-level population is largely
    observed? \textbf{Partially answered}: the reported-served series in
    Section~\ref{sec:results} runs through the anchored period; extending it
    to the present with the current SDWIS snapshot is future work
    (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).
  \item[Q3. No-coal counterfactual.] If all coal mining were removed from the
    network, how many people would \emph{no longer} be downstream of coal?
    This is a descriptive accounting counterfactual, not a causal
    water-quality claim. \textbf{Not yet run} --- set out in
    Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual} as the design for future work.
  \item[Q4. Environmental justice.] Among the exposed population, what is the
    demographic composition (race/ethnicity, income, age), and how does it
    compare to the non-exposed population? \textbf{Not yet run} --- set out
    in Section~\ref{sec:ej} as the design for future work.
\end{description}

The output of the exercise is a set of \emph{counts and distributions}, not a
regression coefficient. This matters methodologically: an aggregate headcount
is far more forgiving of system-level measurement error than a regression
control would be, because idiosyncratic errors across systems tend to cancel
in the sum --- a claim the validation exercises of Section~\ref{sec:validation}
test directly rather than assume.

\section{Institutional background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Community water systems and \PWSID.}
A community water system is a utility that supplies drinking water to the same
resident population year-round (a municipal utility, a rural water district, a
mobile-home park, etc.). Every public water system in the United States
carries a unique identifier, the \PWSID, assigned in the Safe Drinking Water
Information System (SDWIS), the federal regulatory database maintained by the
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). A key administrative field,
\texttt{POPULATION\_SERVED\_COUNT}, records the estimated resident population
the system serves. Two features of this field drive the entire methodology:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item It is a \emph{snapshot}, not a time series. EPA overwrites the value
    as systems file updates, so a single download yields one number per
    system, dated to the download, with no history.
  \item It is a \emph{reported} quantity (the population the utility claims to
    serve), which need not equal the residential population living inside the
    system's service-area boundary --- some residents use private wells, and
    some systems serve transient or commercial demand. The gap between the
    two is central below.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Watersheds and the ``downstream-of-mine'' classification.}
Surface-water flow is organized by the U.S. Geological Survey into nested
watershed units; the finest standard unit used here is the twelve-digit
hydrologic unit code (HUC12), a sub-watershed averaging roughly $100$\,km$^2$.
Each HUC12 records the identity of the single HUC12 it drains into (its
\texttt{tohuc}), so the HUC12 layer forms a directed flow network. A CWS is
matched to the HUC12 containing its intake. Using mine locations (from the
Mine Safety and Health Administration, MSHA) placed on the same network, a
HUC12 --- and the systems drawing from it --- is classified as \emph{downstream
of a mine} in year $t$ if an active mine sits in any HUC12 that flows into it.
The target set for this exercise is narrower than that general classification:
systems that are \emph{ever}, over 1983--2024, one HUC12 \emph{directly}
downstream of an active mine, with no intake in a mining HUC12 itself. This is
the same \texttt{minehuc\_downstream\_of\_mine} classification used elsewhere
in the coal-mining/drinking-water project, so the population weights built
here attach to exactly the sample the project's instrumental-variables
analysis is estimated on. The annual roster $\mathcal{D}_t$ for this set is an
\emph{input} to the present exercise, not something it constructs.

\paragraph{Why this is hard.}
The roster $\mathcal{D}_t$ is known annually, but the quantity we wish to sum
over it --- $S_{i,t}$ --- is observed only at a handful of recent dates
(Section~\ref{sec:data}). The methodological core (Section~\ref{sec:methods})
is a procedure to fill $S_{i,t}$ backward to 1990.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Inputs}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Downstream roster $\mathcal{D}_t$, 1983--2024}, annual, at the
    \PWSID\ level, derived from MSHA mine activity and the HUC12 flow network.
    Includes system existence (entry/exit) by year. 367 systems are ever in
    the roster.
  \item \textbf{Service-area polygons} for CWS: the EPA Community Water System
    service-area boundary layer (SABS), the linchpin that lets us move from
    coarse county proxies to system-specific population
    (Section~\ref{sec:apportion}).
  \item \textbf{Census geography and demography}: tract/block boundary
    polygons and 100\% population counts at the decennial censuses 1990,
    2000, 2010, 2020 (nationwide digital boundary files begin with the 1990
    TIGER/Line release, which is why the study window opens in 1990), and the
    Census Bureau's Population Estimates Program (PEP) annual \emph{county}
    population, used to give the system-level series an annual cadence
    between decennial points (Section~\ref{sec:cadence}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reported population-served anchors}
An \emph{anchor} is a year in which a system's reported population served is
directly observed and linkable to its \PWSID. Table~\ref{tab:backcast_anchors}
inventories the sources used: the SYR2 occurrence databases (1998--2005), the
2006 Community Water System Survey frame, and the SDWIS 2010 and 2011 freezes.
Two features of this set constrain everything downstream. First, each source
supplies \emph{one} inventory value per system, not an annual series --- the
SYR2 databases carry a single \texttt{POPULATION} field stamped on every
sample record, so SYR2 yields one anchor per system, dated to its first sample
year, not an eight-year panel. Second, no anchor of any kind exists before
1998: the 1995 and 2000 Community Water System Surveys are de-identified
stratified samples that cannot be attached to a \PWSID, and the 2005 SDWIS
``freeze'' is an 87-row summary pivot with no system records. The 1990--1997
window is therefore anchored only by extrapolation, and this is flagged
throughout. The modern SDWIS snapshot is deliberately excluded from the anchor
set: its \texttt{POPULATION\_SERVED\_COUNT} is a single current-day value
broadcast across all years, the exact artifact this exercise exists to
remove.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The estimand and the identity behind the reconstruction}
\label{sec:identity}

For a system $i$ with exposure polygon $A_i$, define two distinct population
concepts:
\begin{align}
G_{i,t} &= \text{residential population living inside } A_i \text{ in year } t,
  &&\text{(from Census)} \label{eq:G-def}\\
S_{i,t} &= \text{population the system reports serving in year } t.
  &&\text{(from SDWIS/anchors)} \label{eq:S-def}
\end{align}
They are linked by a system-specific \emph{capture ratio}
$r_{i,t}\equiv S_{i,t}/G_{i,t}$, which absorbs everything that makes reported
service differ from polygon residence: private-well penetration, transient and
commercial demand, and imprecise boundaries. The object recovered directly is
$G_{i,t}$, the residential backbone, which the areal apportionment identifies
without any anchoring in reported figures, and which is arguably the right
notion of exposure in its own right: it counts the people living in the
territory the system draws water for, whether or not the utility books them as
customers.

We report \emph{both}. Steps 1--2 (Sections~\ref{sec:apportion}--\ref{sec:cadence})
build the residential backbone $G_{i,t}$ for every system and year; Step 3
(Section~\ref{sec:backcast-step3}) calibrates $r_{i,t}$ against observed
reported population at the anchor years and forms
$\hat S_{i,t}=\hat r_{i,t}\hat G_{i,t}$; Step 4 (Section~\ref{sec:exposure})
aggregates over the year-specific roster. The two measures bracket the truth
and, as Section~\ref{sec:results} shows, tell materially different stories
about the 1990--2005 period --- a divergence that is itself one of the more
useful outputs of the exercise, because it isolates how much of the apparent
change in exposure is an artifact of the coarser geography used for some
systems.

For readers new to this style of estimate: the logic mirrors how demographers
``backcast'' missing population. A slowly moving, mechanically determined
quantity (residents inside a fixed boundary, $G$) serves as a deterministic
backbone, and the comparatively small, system-specific deviation from it
($r$) is the only thing that must be modeled.

\subsection{Scope: which systems, and which polygon}
\label{sec:scope}

Each of the 367 target systems needs a polygon into which Census population
can be apportioned. Polygons are assigned in two tiers, in order of
preference:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Service-area polygon.} The EPA SABS layer gives the actual
    territory the utility serves. 239 systems match.
  \item \textbf{County polygon.} For systems with no SABS boundary, the county
    reported in \texttt{SDWA\allowbreak\_GEOGRAPHIC\allowbreak\_AREAS} is used
    as the exposure geography. 127 systems fall into this tier, spanning 57
    distinct counties.
\end{enumerate}
One system matches neither and is dropped (Table~\ref{tab:backcast_recovery}).
The tiers are not interchangeable and are kept separate in every table below:
a county polygon is far larger than the service area nested inside it, so
county-tier systems mechanically carry a much larger population than they
truly serve. They are informative about \emph{trends}, since a county's growth
rate is a reasonable proxy for its utilities' growth rate, but their
\emph{levels} are upper bounds.

\subsection{Step 1: apportioning Census population into the polygons}
\label{sec:apportion}

A Census block rarely coincides with a service-area boundary, so population is
moved into $A_i$ by \emph{population-weighted areal interpolation}. For each
decennial vintage $t\in\{1990,2000,2010,2020\}$, intersect $A_i$ with that
vintage's block layer and sum block population weighted by the share of each
block's area falling inside the polygon:
\begin{equation}
G_{i,t} \;=\; \sum_{b} \texttt{pop}_{b,t}\, w_{b,i,t},
\qquad
w_{b,i,t} \;=\;
  \frac{\operatorname{area}\!\left(b \cap A_i\right)}{\operatorname{area}(b)} .
\label{eq:apportion}
\end{equation}
Blocks are the finest Census unit and carry 100\% counts at each decennial,
which keeps $w_{b,i,t}$ accurate. All geometry is handled in EPSG:5070 (Albers
equal area) so that the areas in \eqref{eq:apportion} are undistorted.

Because the target polygon $A_i$ is held fixed while the block layer is
re-intersected at each vintage, the usual obstacle to long-run Census
work --- boundary redefinition every decade --- does not arise: no
tract-to-tract or block-to-block crosswalk is ever needed. The same overlay
also yields the share of each system's population drawn from each county,
$\theta_{i,c,t_0} = G_{i,t_0}^{(c)} / G_{i,t_0}$, which feeds Step 2. Just
under a quarter of service areas (57 of 239) straddle a county line, so this
blend is not a formality.

\subsection{Step 2: from four decennial snapshots to an annual series}
\label{sec:cadence}

Equation~\eqref{eq:apportion} delivers population only in Census years. To
reach annual frequency, each system's polygon population rides its county's
observed annual trajectory, taken from PEP. Let
$P_{c(i),t}=\sum_c \theta_{i,c,t_0} P_{c,t}$ be the population-weighted blend
of the counties the system overlaps. Starting from the most recent decennial
anchor $t_0$, the annual value is defined recursively by applying the
county's gross growth factor:
\begin{equation}
\hat G_{i,t} \;=\; \hat G_{i,t-1}\cdot\frac{P_{c(i),t}}{P_{c(i),t-1}},
\qquad
\hat G_{i,t_0} \;=\; G_{i,t_0} .
\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
The recursion telescopes, since the cumulative product of annual growth
factors collapses to the ratio of county populations at the endpoints:
\begin{equation}
\hat G_{i,t} \;=\; G_{i,t_0}\cdot\frac{P_{c(i),t}}{P_{c(i),t_0}}
  \;=\; \kappa_{i,t_0}\,P_{c(i),t},
\qquad
\kappa_{i,t_0} \equiv \frac{G_{i,t_0}}{P_{c(i),t_0}} .
\label{eq:chain-closed}
\end{equation}
Written this way, the content of the rule is transparent: it holds the
system's \emph{coverage ratio} $\kappa$ --- the share of its county's
population that lives inside the service area --- fixed at the opening
decennial value, and lets the level follow the county thereafter. The single
substantive assumption is that $\kappa$ is constant within a decade. At each
decennial the level is reset to the freshly observed apportionment and
chaining resumes, so the assumption is only ever imposed over a ten-year span,
never over the full window; the reset introduces a small discontinuity at
decade boundaries equal to the decade's realized drift in $\kappa$, which is
the honest price of not benchmarking to the closing decennial. This is exactly
the degenerate case of a full two-endpoint benchmarking rule in which the
closing-decennial coverage ratio happens to equal the opening one; here it is
imposed rather than estimated, and Section~\ref{sec:validation} tests the cost
of that choice directly (leave-one-decade-out).

The 1990 floor is binding and cannot be pushed back: nationwide digital block
boundaries begin with the 1990 TIGER/Line release. 1980 tract polygons exist
only for already-tracted metropolitan areas and would introduce exactly the
urban selection this exercise is trying to avoid. The roster extends back to
1983, so 1983--1989 is left unrecovered rather than extrapolated.

\subsection{Step 3: the reported layer, calibrating $r$}
\label{sec:backcast-step3}

The backbone above counts residents. To recover what utilities actually
report serving, the capture ratio $r_{i,t}=S_{i,t}/G_{i,t}$ must be calibrated
wherever $S$ is observed and carried elsewhere.

\paragraph{The ratio is only meaningful for one tier.}
Table~\ref{tab:backcast_ratio} reports the ratio's distribution, and the split
by geography tier is decisive. For service-area systems $r$ behaves exactly as
the framework assumes: it centers at $1.53$, slightly above one, consistent
with utilities serving some demand beyond their resident population, and the
median system's ratio moves only $1.15\times$ between its earliest and latest
anchor. That drift statistic is the direct test of the constant- and
interpolated-$r$ assumptions, and it passes. For county-tier systems it does
not: the denominator $G$ is an entire county's population, so
$r\approx 0.001$ measures the utility's share of its county rather than any
capture rate. Multiplying such a ratio back by $G$ would merely return the
reported number while discarding the backbone entirely --- a circular
operation. The two tiers are therefore treated differently: service-area
systems carry the ratio, $\hat S_{i,t}=\hat r_{i,t}\hat G_{i,t}$, while
county-tier systems take their reported population as the \emph{level} and
the county's growth factor as the \emph{trend},
$\hat S_{i,t}=S_{i,a}\bigl(\hat G_{i,t}/\hat G_{i,a}\bigr)$. Within the
anchored range $r$ is interpolated linearly between anchors and held flat
outside it; the five systems with no anchor at all take the same-tier median
and are flagged as least reliable.

\input{sum/backcast_ratio}

\paragraph{Wholesale de-duplication.}
A system that sells water to another reports a population its buyer also
reports, so summing both double-counts those residents. Applying the 2010
Buyers/Sellers crosswalk identifies 39 buyer--seller pairs in which both sides
are downstream, implicating 27 sellers, which are removed from $E^S_t$ before
aggregation.

\subsection{Step 4: aggregating over the year-specific roster}
\label{sec:exposure}

The downstream set $\mathcal{D}_t$ is known annually and changes as mines open
and close. Total exposed population under each concept is
\begin{align}
\text{Residential exposure:}\quad & E^{G}_t \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_t} \hat G_{i,t}, \\
\text{Reported-served exposure:}\quad & E^{S}_t \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_t} \hat r_{i,t}\,\hat G_{i,t}.
\end{align}
Summing over the year-specific roster rather than a fixed set matters: it lets
the measure capture systems entering and leaving the downstream set --- the
extensive margin --- and not only changes in population per system.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Coverage.}
Table~\ref{tab:backcast_recovery} reports what was recovered. Of the 367
systems ever one HUC12 directly downstream of coal mining, 366 (99.7\%)
receive a complete population series --- 239 through their own service-area
polygon and 127 through a county fallback, spanning 18 states. Only one
system is lost for want of any usable geography. Each recovered system has an
uninterrupted annual observation for every year from 1990 to 2024, giving a
balanced panel of 12,810 system-years with no missing values.

\input{sum/backcast_recovery}

\paragraph{Distribution of population.}
Table~\ref{tab:backcast_popsum} summarizes the recovered population. The
pooled distribution is strongly right-skewed, with a mean of 53,947 against a
median of 3,993, and bimodal --- an artifact of the two-tier geography rather
than a feature of the data. Read separately, the tiers are coherent:
service-area systems have a median of 480 residents against a mean of 10,234,
consistent with the many small rural systems that dominate the downstream
sample, while county-tier systems have a mean of 136,209, reflecting that they
carry an entire county. The service-area figures are the ones to take at face
value. The cross-sections in Panel B, which hold the set of systems fixed at
all 366, show how little the underlying population moved: the median
recovered system had 3,815 residents in 1990 and 4,019 in 2005.

\input{sum/backcast_popsum}

\paragraph{Change from 1990 to 2005.}
Table~\ref{tab:backcast_change} reports the change from the earliest recovered
year to 2005, and the split between panels is the substantive result. Holding
the set of systems fixed (Panel A), residential population downstream of
mining is essentially flat: 19.32 million in 1990 against 19.61 million in
2005, a rise of 1.47\% over fifteen years. The median system grew 0.48\%, and
47.3\% of systems shrank --- close to an even split. The mean percentage
change should be disregarded as a measure of central tendency: it is driven by
a handful of very small systems growing from a near-zero base. Panel B, which
sums over the year-specific roster $\mathcal{D}_t$, shows a much larger
decline (residential exposure falls by more than half) because the roster
itself contracts from 340 systems in 1990 to 278 in 2005.

\input{sum/backcast_change_9005}

\paragraph{Result: the two exposure measures diverge sharply.}
Table~\ref{tab:backcast_reported_exposure} sets $E^{S}_t$ against $E^{G}_t$,
and the contrast is the most consequential finding of the whole exercise.
Reported exposure falls from 2.28 million in 1990 to 2.12 million in 2005, a
decline of $7.3$\%. Residential exposure over the same roster falls by more
than half. The ratio $E^{S}_t/E^{G}_t$ climbs steadily from $0.159$ to
$0.321$, the signature of a compositional shift rather than a population
change.

The decomposition explains it. Of the 62 systems that leave the downstream
roster between 1990 and 2005, 61 are county-tier and only one is a service
area, and their median reported population is 60 people: small rural
utilities that happened to lack a service-area boundary and so were assigned
their whole county's population in the residential measure. They contribute
7.92 million residents to $E^{G}_{1990}$ but only 50,436 reported customers.
The steep fall in $E^{G}_t$ is thus substantially an artifact of the county
fallback geography, not evidence that millions of people ceased to be
exposed. This does not overturn the direction of the result --- exposure
still declines on both measures, and the roster still contracts from 340
systems to 278 --- but it changes the magnitude by an order of magnitude and
reassigns its interpretation. $E^{S}_t$ is the more credible measure of how
the exposed customer base actually evolved: the number of \emph{systems}
downstream of active mining fell sharply while the number of \emph{people}
they served fell modestly, because the systems that dropped out were small.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Six checks discipline the pipeline, all run against live data.

First, an \textbf{external check on the overlay itself}. For the 127
county-tier systems the apportionment target is a whole county, so
\eqref{eq:apportion} should reproduce that county's independently measured
population. Across all 508 county-tier system-years the ratio of apportioned
to PEP county population lies in $[0.987,1.036]$ with mean $0.9997$: the areal
machinery is not introducing meaningful bias.

Second, \textbf{internal consistency}: the county shares $\theta$ sum to one
for all 1,464 system-vintage rows; the areal weights $w$ lie in $[0,1]$ with
no missing values; and the annual panel is exactly balanced at
$366\times 35=12{,}810$ rows with no nulls or zeros.

Third, a \textbf{plausibility check on movement}. Within decades the series
moves smoothly, with level resets only at decennial boundaries, as
\eqref{eq:chain-closed} implies. Eight system-years show polygon population
exceeding a single county's population; all are service areas spanning
several counties, where summing across counties legitimately exceeds any one
of them.

Fourth, the \textbf{ratio-drift diagnostic} in Table~\ref{tab:backcast_ratio}:
for the 235 service-area systems with more than one anchor, the median
system's capture ratio moves $1.15\times$ between its lowest and highest
anchor, and the 90th percentile moves $2.19\times$. This measures directly how
much the constant- and interpolated-$r$ assumptions strain, rather than
assuming the question away.

Fifth, \textbf{leave-one-decade-out} on the $G_{i,t}$ backbone
(Table~\ref{tab:backcast_lodo}): each of the two interior decennials, 2000 and
2010, is predicted from each of its two neighbors by PEP-scaling
\eqref{eq:chain-closed} and compared to the true block apportionment, with no
reported figure anywhere in the loop. For the service-area tier --- the
substantive test of the $\kappa$-constant assumption, since the county tier
moves with the county PEP series by construction --- aggregate error across
the four target$\times$direction combinations is small, $-1.0\%$ to $+1.9\%$,
while median per-system absolute error is $7$--$12\%$ and $70$--$78\%$ of
systems land within $25\%$. A decade of $\kappa$ drift is enough to move
individual systems but not enough to bias the sum, because drift is not
one-signed across systems.

\input{sum/backcast_lodo}

Sixth, \textbf{leave-one-anchor-out} on the reported-served layer $\hat
S_{i,t}$ (Table~\ref{tab:backcast_loao}): each of three eras of reported
anchors --- 2010--2011, 2006, and the SYR2 era (1998--2005) --- is withheld in
turn and re-predicted from the remaining eras using the production estimator
of Section~\ref{sec:backcast-step3}, with folds built as whole eras rather
than individual anchors because 2010 and 2011 are near-duplicates
(correlation $0.998$; $90.8\%$ of systems report an identical value in both
years) and a per-anchor leave-one-out would trivially predict one from the
other. Service-area aggregate error ranges $-21.5\%$ to $+7.3\%$ across the
three folds, with median per-system absolute error $12.5$--$14.8\%$; the
hardest fold, predicting the 1998--2005 anchors from only 2006 and
2010--2011 (a gap of up to 12 years), has the largest error, as expected.
County-tier aggregate error is larger in magnitude ($-26.1\%$ to $+22.6\%$)
but its median per-system error is smaller ($1.8$--$6.1\%$), consistent with
the level-and-county-growth carry being accurate for most systems but
occasionally missing a large one. As with LODO, aggregate accuracy exceeds
per-system accuracy throughout --- the reading the exercise was designed to
support rather than assume.

\section{Q3: the no-coal counterfactual (not yet run)}
\label{sec:counterfactual}

``If all coal disappeared'' can be operationalized mechanically. Let
$\mathcal{D}_t$ be the factual downstream set (mines active as observed) and
$\mathcal{D}^{0}_t$ the set recomputed after \emph{emptying the mine layer}
from the flow network. With no active mines, no system is downstream of a
mine, so $\mathcal{D}^{0}_t=\varnothing$ and the relieved population is simply
$R_t = \sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_t}\hat S_{i,t} = E^S_t$, already computed in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Two refinements would make this more informative
than a single number, and are the design for future work:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Intensity weighting.} Rather than a binary downstream
    indicator, characterize exposure by the number of upstream mines,
    upstream production, or upstream coal sulfur content, and report the
    relieved population by intensity tier.
  \item \textbf{Active vs.\ legacy coal.} Removing only \emph{active} mines
    differs from removing all coal influence, since abandoned mine lands can
    continue to affect water; the legacy variant would be reported as a
    bound.
\end{enumerate}
This is descriptive accounting --- a count of who is currently downstream ---
and is explicitly \emph{not} a claim that water quality would improve by a
measured amount.

\section{Q4: environmental-justice analysis (not yet run)}
\label{sec:ej}

For each downstream system, the same polygon weights $w_{b,i,t}$ used for
population (Section~\ref{sec:apportion}) can be applied to Census tract
demographic shares (race/ethnicity, median household income, age, and poverty
status) to obtain the demographic composition of the population served. The
planned analysis would then: (i) compare the exposed population's composition
to that of the non-exposed CWS population at each Census year, testing whether
disadvantaged groups are over-represented downstream of coal; (ii) track these
gaps over 1990--2020 to see whether the burden's demographic profile shifted;
and (iii) summarize disproportionality with standard EJ measures
(population-weighted exposure shares by group; optionally a
concentration/dissimilarity index). Because demographic \emph{composition} is
far more stable and better measured than the population \emph{level}, this is
expected to be the most robust output of the exercise once run, depending
least on the backcasting assumptions validated in Section~\ref{sec:validation}.

\section{Optional machine-learning robustness (future work)}
\label{sec:ml}

The procedure above is deliberately transparent and, for aggregate headcounts,
the validation in Section~\ref{sec:validation} suggests it is adequate. As a
robustness check, the simple $r$ rule and PEP-shape interpolation could be
replaced with a supervised-learning predictor (e.g.\ a neural network or
gradient-boosted trees) trained on the anchor years to map exogenous features
(Census county/place population, demographics, system type, source water) to
population served, in the spirit of the demographic backcasting literature.
Any such model should deliberately \emph{exclude} SDWA violation and
enforcement variables from the feature set, so that the exposure measure is
not mechanically a function of the regulatory outcomes studied elsewhere in
the project. Given that the deterministic backbone already explains the bulk
of the variation (Section~\ref{sec:validation}), a machine-learning layer is
expected to refine rather than redefine the estimates, and would be reported
as a sensitivity, not the headline.

\section{Threats to validity and limitations}
\label{sec:threats}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Pre-1998 extrapolation.} No system-level anchor exists before
    SYR2, so 1990--1997 exposure is a Census-residential extrapolation with
    $r$ held flat at its earliest observed value. This is reported as such
    and the better-anchored 1998--2005 segment is leaned on for Q1.
  \item \textbf{1990 polygon floor.} Nationwide tract/block polygons start in
    1990; the study cannot extend earlier without metro-only, lower-quality
    1980 geography, and the roster's 1983--1989 years are left unrecovered.
  \item \textbf{Service-polygon accuracy.} If a polygon poorly delineates the
    true service area, $G$ is mismeasured; the $r$ calibration partly
    corrects this, and the ratio-drift diagnostic (Section~\ref{sec:validation})
    flags the worst cases.
  \item \textbf{Capture-ratio non-stationarity.} Holding/interpolating $r$
    assumes a roughly stable captured share; annexation and consolidation
    violate this. Measured drift bounds the error.
  \item \textbf{County-tier levels are upper bounds.} 127 of 366 recovered
    systems carry their entire county's population rather than a
    system-specific footprint, which mechanically inflates $E^G_t$ relative
    to $E^S_t$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}) and is why the reported-served
    series is preferred for levels.
  \item \textbf{Reporting comparability.} ``Population served'' is defined
    slightly differently across SYR2, CWSS, and the SDWIS freezes;
    definitional drift can masquerade as real change and is checked at
    overlapping years.
  \item \textbf{Aggregation cuts both ways.} Headcounts are robust because
    errors cancel (Section~\ref{sec:validation} confirms this directly); any
    \emph{system-level} statement is far less reliable than the totals.
  \item \textbf{Q2, Q3, Q4 are not yet executed}, only designed
    (Sections~\ref{sec:questions}, \ref{sec:counterfactual}, \ref{sec:ej}).
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary}

The known annual roster of systems downstream of coal mining ($\mathcal{D}_t$)
is combined with service-area polygons and Census data to reconstruct, system
by system, the population each serves, via the identity $S=r\cdot G$: Census
apportionment supplies the residential backbone $G$ for every year back to
1990, and the recent reported anchors (SYR2, CWSS 2006, and the 2010/2011
freezes) calibrate the capture ratio $r$. Applied to the 367 systems ever
downstream of coal mining, the procedure recovers 366 complete population
series and shows that residential and reported-served exposure diverge
sharply --- a divergence traced to the county-fallback tier rather than a
mining effect. Two independent leave-one-out validations, on the $\kappa$-constant
chaining assumption and on the capture-ratio calibration, both show that
aggregate exposure totals are accurate even where individual systems are
noisy. The design's honest weak points are the unanchored 1990--1997 window
and the upper-bound nature of county-tier levels; its most robust output,
once run, would be the demographic composition underlying Q4, which barely
depends on the backcasting assumptions at all.
