\subsection{Recovering the population served by downstream utilities}
\label{sec:backcasting}

The population that a community water system serves is the natural weight for
any statement about how much drinking-water exposure to coal mining actually
matters. It is also the quantity the Safe Drinking Water Information System
(SDWIS) records worst. \texttt{POPULATION\_SERVED\_COUNT} is a snapshot rather
than a time series: the EPA overwrites the field as systems file updates, so a
download yields one number per system, dated to the download, with no history.
Statements about the population exposed to coal mining between 1985 and 2005
therefore cannot be made from SDWIS directly --- they would attribute a 2024
population to a 1990 system.

This section describes a procedure that recovers a system-level population
series back to 1990 from Census geography, and reports what it delivers. The
logic is deliberately mechanical: a slowly moving, administratively determined
quantity --- the number of residents living inside a fixed service-area boundary
--- serves as the backbone, and nothing about the regulatory outcomes studied
elsewhere in the paper enters the construction.

\subsubsection{The estimand and the identity behind the reconstruction}

For a system $i$ with exposure polygon $A_i$, define two distinct population
concepts:
%
\begin{align}
G_{i,t} &= \text{residential population living inside } A_i \text{ in year } t
  &&\text{(Census)}, \\
S_{i,t} &= \text{population the system reports serving in year } t
  &&\text{(SDWIS)} .
\end{align}
%
They are linked by a system-specific capture ratio $r_{i,t}\equiv S_{i,t}/G_{i,t}$,
which absorbs everything that makes reported service differ from polygon
residence: private-well penetration, transient and commercial demand, and
imprecise boundaries. The object recovered here is $G_{i,t}$, the residential
backbone, which is what the areal apportionment identifies directly and which
requires no anchoring in reported figures. It is also arguably the right notion
of exposure: it counts the people living in the territory the system draws water
for, whether or not the utility books them as customers.

I report \emph{both}. Steps 1 and 2 build the residential backbone $G_{i,t}$ for
every system and year; Step 4 calibrates $r_{i,t}$ against observed reported
population at the years where it exists and forms
$\hat S_{i,t}=\hat r_{i,t}\hat G_{i,t}$. The two bracket the truth and, as
Section~\ref{sec:backcast-step3} shows, they tell materially different stories
about the 1990--2005 period --- a divergence that is itself the most useful
output of the exercise, because it isolates how much of the apparent change in
exposure is an artifact of the coarser geography used for some systems.

\subsubsection{Scope: which systems}

The target set is the systems that are \emph{ever} one HUC12 directly downstream
of coal mining --- at least one intake in a HUC12 immediately downstream of a
HUC12 with an active mine, and no intake in a mining HUC12 itself. This is the
same \texttt{minehuc\_downstream\_of\_mine} classification used in the
instrumental-variables analysis, so the population weights attach to exactly the
sample the second stage is estimated on. There are 367 such systems over
1983--2024.

Each system needs a polygon into which Census population can be apportioned.
These are assigned in two tiers, in order of preference:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Service-area polygon.} The EPA Community Water System service-area
    boundary layer (SABS) gives the actual territory the utility serves. 239
    systems match.
  \item \textbf{County polygon.} For systems with no SABS boundary, the county
    reported in \texttt{SDWA\_GEOGRAPHIC\_AREAS} is used as the exposure geography.
    127 systems fall into this tier, spanning 57 distinct counties.
\end{enumerate}

One system matches neither and is dropped. The tiers are not interchangeable and
I keep them separate in every table below: a county polygon is far larger than
the service area nested inside it, so county-tier systems mechanically carry a
much larger population than they truly serve. They are informative about
\emph{trends}, since a county's growth rate is a reasonable proxy for its
utilities' growth rate, but their \emph{levels} are upper bounds.

\subsubsection{Step 1: apportioning Census population into the polygons}

A Census block rarely coincides with a service-area boundary, so population is
moved into $A_i$ by areal interpolation. For each decennial vintage
$t\in\{1990,2000,2010,2020\}$ I intersect $A_i$ with that vintage's block layer
and sum block population weighted by the share of each block's area falling
inside the polygon:
%
\begin{equation}
G_{i,t} \;=\; \sum_{b} \texttt{pop}_{b,t}\, w_{b,i,t},
\qquad
w_{b,i,t} \;=\;
  \frac{\operatorname{area}\!\left(b \cap A_i\right)}{\operatorname{area}(b)} .
\label{eq:apportion}
\end{equation}
%
Blocks are the finest Census unit and carry 100\% counts at each decennial, which
keeps $w_{b,i,t}$ accurate. All geometry is handled in EPSG:5070 (Albers equal
area) so that the areas in \eqref{eq:apportion} are undistorted.

Because the target polygon $A_i$ is held fixed while the block layer is
re-intersected at each vintage, the usual obstacle to long-run Census
work --- boundary redefinition every decade --- does not arise. No
tract-to-tract or block-to-block crosswalk is ever needed. The weights are
computed once and reused, which is what makes the same machinery available for
the demographic apportionment.

The same overlay also yields the share of each system's population drawn from
each county,
$\theta_{i,c,t_0} = G_{i,t_0}^{(c)} / G_{i,t_0}$, which feeds Step 2. Just under a
quarter of service areas (57 of 239) straddle a county line, so this blend is not
a formality.

\subsubsection{Step 2: from four decennial snapshots to an annual series}

Equation \eqref{eq:apportion} delivers population only in Census years. To reach
annual frequency I let each system's polygon population ride its county's
observed annual trajectory, taken from the Census Bureau's Population Estimates
Program (PEP). Let $P_{c(i),t}=\sum_c \theta_{i,c,t_0} P_{c,t}$ be the
population-weighted blend of the counties the system overlaps. Starting from the
most recent decennial anchor $t_0$, the annual value is defined recursively by
applying the county's gross growth factor:
%
\begin{equation}
\hat G_{i,t} \;=\; \hat G_{i,t-1}\cdot\frac{P_{c(i),t}}{P_{c(i),t-1}},
\qquad
\hat G_{i,t_0} \;=\; G_{i,t_0} .
\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
%
The recursion telescopes, since the cumulative product of annual growth factors
collapses to the ratio of county populations at the endpoints:
%
\begin{equation}
\hat G_{i,t} \;=\; G_{i,t_0}\cdot\frac{P_{c(i),t}}{P_{c(i),t_0}}
  \;=\; \kappa_{i,t_0}\,P_{c(i),t},
\qquad
\kappa_{i,t_0} \equiv \frac{G_{i,t_0}}{P_{c(i),t_0}} .
\label{eq:chain-closed}
\end{equation}
%
Written this way the content of the rule is transparent. It holds the system's
\emph{coverage ratio} $\kappa$ --- the share of its county's population that
lives inside the service area --- fixed at the opening decennial value, and lets
the level follow the county thereafter. The single substantive assumption is that
$\kappa$ is constant within a decade. At each decennial the level is reset to the
freshly observed apportionment and chaining resumes, so the assumption is only
ever imposed over a ten-year span, never over the full window. The reset does
introduce a small discontinuity at decade boundaries equal to the decade's
realized drift in $\kappa$; this is visible in the series and is the honest price
of not benchmarking to the closing decennial.

Note that \eqref{eq:chain} runs in both directions from an anchor in the ordinary
sense that 1990--1999 is carried forward from the 1990 anchor. The 1990 floor is
binding and cannot be pushed back: nationwide digital block boundaries begin with
the 1990 TIGER/Line release. 1980 tract polygons exist only for already-tracted
metropolitan areas and would introduce exactly the urban selection this exercise
is trying to avoid. The roster extends back to 1983, so 1983--1989 is left
unrecovered rather than extrapolated.

\subsubsection{Step 3: aggregating over the year-specific roster}

The downstream set $\mathcal{D}_t$ is known annually and changes as mines open
and close. Total exposed residential population is
%
\begin{equation}
E^{G}_t \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_t} \hat G_{i,t} .
\label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
%
Summing over the year-specific roster rather than a fixed set matters: it lets
the measure capture systems entering and leaving the downstream set --- the
extensive margin --- and not only changes in population per system. As the
results show, that distinction carries essentially the entire movement in
$E^{G}_t$ --- though Section~\ref{sec:backcast-step3} shows it carries far less
of the movement in the reported-served measure.

\subsubsection{Step 4: the reported layer, calibrating $r$}
\label{sec:backcast-step3}

The backbone above counts residents. To recover what utilities actually report
serving, the capture ratio $r_{i,t}=S_{i,t}/G_{i,t}$ must be calibrated wherever
$S$ is observed and carried elsewhere.

\paragraph{Anchors.}
An \emph{anchor} is a year in which a system's reported population served is
directly observed and linkable to its \texttt{PWSID}. Four sources supply them,
inventoried in Table~\ref{tab:backcast_anchors}: the SYR2 occurrence databases
(1998--2005), the 2006 Community Water System Survey frame, and the SDWIS 2010
and 2011 freezes. Two features of this set constrain everything downstream.

First, each source supplies \emph{one} inventory value per system, not an annual
series. The SYR2 databases carry a \texttt{POPULATION} field stamped on every
sample record, and it does not vary across a system's 1998--2005 samples for any
of the 49,473 systems present --- so SYR2 yields a single anchor, dated to the
system's first sample year, rather than an eight-year panel. Second, no anchor of
any kind exists before 1998. The 1995 and 2000 Community Water System Surveys are
de-identified stratified samples that cannot be attached to a \texttt{PWSID}, and
the 2005 SDWIS ``freeze'' is an 87-row summary pivot with no system records. The
1990--1997 window is therefore anchored only by extrapolation, and I flag it as
such.

I deliberately exclude the modern SDWIS snapshot from the anchor set. Its
\texttt{POPULATION\_SERVED\_COUNT} is a single 2024Q4 value broadcast across all
years, which is the exact artifact this exercise exists to remove; admitting it
would reintroduce the problem under a new name.

\paragraph{The ratio is only meaningful for one tier.}
Table~\ref{tab:backcast_ratio} reports the ratio's distribution, and the split by
geography tier is decisive. For service-area systems $r$ behaves exactly as the
framework assumes: it centers at $1.53$, slightly above one, consistent with
utilities serving some demand beyond their resident population, and the median
system's ratio moves only $1.15\times$ between its earliest and latest anchor.
That drift statistic is the direct test of the constant- and interpolated-$r$
assumptions, and it passes.

For county-tier systems it does not. There the denominator $G$ is an entire
county's population, so $r\approx 0.001$ measures the utility's share of its
county rather than any capture rate. Multiplying such a ratio back by $G$ would
merely return the reported number while discarding the backbone entirely --- a
circular operation that would quietly make $E^{S}_t$ a sum of raw reported values
for 127 of 366 systems. I therefore treat the tiers differently: service-area
systems carry the ratio, $\hat S_{i,t}=\hat r_{i,t}\hat G_{i,t}$, while
county-tier systems take their reported population as the \emph{level} and the
county's growth factor as the \emph{trend},
$\hat S_{i,t}=S_{i,a}\bigl(\hat G_{i,t}/\hat G_{i,a}\bigr)$. Within the anchored
range $r$ is interpolated linearly between anchors and held flat outside it; the
five systems with no anchor at all take the same-tier median and are flagged as
least reliable.

\paragraph{Wholesale de-duplication.}
A system that sells water to another reports a population its buyer also reports,
so summing both double-counts those residents. Applying the 2010 Buyers/Sellers
crosswalk identifies 39 buyer--seller pairs in which both sides are downstream,
implicating 27 sellers. These are removed from $E^{S}_t$ before aggregation.

\paragraph{Result: the two measures diverge sharply.}
Table~\ref{tab:backcast_reported_exposure} sets $E^{S}_t$ against $E^{G}_t$, and
the contrast is the most consequential finding of the whole exercise. Reported
exposure falls from 2.28 million in 1990 to 2.12 million in 2005, a decline of
$7.3$\%. Residential exposure over the same systems falls by more than half. The
ratio $E^{S}_t/E^{G}_t$ climbs steadily from $0.159$ to $0.321$, which is the
signature of a compositional shift rather than a population change.

The decomposition explains it. Of the 62 systems that leave the downstream roster
between 1990 and 2005, \textbf{61 are county-tier and only one is a service
area}, and their median reported population is 60 people. These are small rural
utilities that happened to lack a service-area boundary and so were assigned
their whole county's population in the residential measure. They contribute 7.92
million residents to $E^{G}_{1990}$ but only 50,436 reported customers. The steep
fall in $E^{G}_t$ is thus substantially an artifact of the county fallback
geography, not evidence that millions of people ceased to be exposed.

This does not overturn the direction of the result --- exposure still declines on
both measures, and the roster still contracts from 340 systems to 278 --- but it
changes the magnitude by an order of magnitude and reassigns its interpretation.
$E^{S}_t$ is the more credible measure of how the exposed customer base actually
evolved, and the honest summary is that the number of \emph{systems} downstream
of active mining fell sharply while the number of \emph{people} they served fell
modestly, because the systems that dropped out were small.

\subsubsection{Validation}

Four checks discipline the pipeline, all run against live data.

First, an external check on the overlay itself. For the 127 county-tier systems
the apportionment target is a whole county, so \eqref{eq:apportion} should
reproduce that county's independently measured population. Across all 508
county-tier system-years the ratio of apportioned to PEP county population lies
in $[0.987,1.036]$ with mean $0.9997$. The areal machinery is therefore not
introducing meaningful bias.

Second, internal consistency: the county shares $\theta$ sum to one for all 1,464
system-vintage rows; the areal weights $w$ lie in $[0,1]$ with no missing values;
and the annual panel is exactly balanced at $366\times 35=12{,}810$ rows with no
nulls or zeros.

Third, a plausibility check on movement. Within decades the series moves
smoothly, with level resets only at decennial boundaries, as
\eqref{eq:chain-closed} implies. Eight system-years show polygon population
exceeding a single county's population; all are service areas spanning several
counties --- \texttt{KY0310114} spans six, with a largest share of 61\% --- where
summing across counties legitimately exceeds any one of them.

Fourth, the ratio-drift diagnostic in Table~\ref{tab:backcast_ratio}: for the 235
service-area systems with more than one anchor, the median system's capture ratio
moves $1.15\times$ between its lowest and highest anchor, and the 90th percentile
moves $2.19\times$. This measures directly how much the constant- and
interpolated-$r$ assumptions strain, rather than assuming the question away.

Two exercises remain outstanding and are noted as limitations rather than
claimed: a leave-one-decade-out test that predicts the 2000 apportionment by
PEP-scaling from 1990 and 2010 and compares it to the true 2000 block
apportionment, and a leave-one-anchor-out test that withholds a reported anchor
and predicts it from the others.

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{Coverage.}
Table \ref{tab:backcast_recovery} reports what was recovered. Of the 367 systems
ever one HUC12 directly downstream of coal mining, 366 (99.7\%) receive a
complete population series --- 239 through their own service-area polygon and 127
through a county fallback, spanning 18 states. Only one system, \texttt{SC2460011},
is lost for want of any usable geography. Each recovered system has an
uninterrupted annual observation for every year from 1990 to 2024, giving a
balanced panel of 12,810 system-years with no missing values: four decennial
anchor years measured directly from block apportionment and 31 intercensal years
chained from county growth. The years 1983--1989 of the roster are not recovered,
for the reason given above.

\paragraph{Distribution of population.}
Table \ref{tab:backcast_popsum} summarizes the recovered population. The pooled
distribution is strongly right-skewed, with a mean of 53,947 against a median of
3,993, and it is bimodal --- an artifact of the two-tier geography rather than a
feature of the data. Read separately, the tiers are coherent. Service-area
systems have a median of 480 residents against a mean of 10,234, consistent with
the many small rural systems that dominate the downstream sample; a quarter cover
fewer than 61 people. County-tier systems have a mean of 136,209, reflecting that
they carry an entire county. The service-area figures are the ones to take at
face value.

The cross-sections in Panel B, which hold the set of systems fixed at all 366,
show how little the underlying population moved: the median recovered system had
3,815 residents in 1990 and 4,019 in 2005, and the whole distribution is close to
static over the study window.

\paragraph{Change from 1990 to 2005.}
Table \ref{tab:backcast_change} reports the change from the earliest recovered
year to 2005, and the split between panels is the substantive result.

Holding the set of systems fixed (Panel A), residential population downstream of
mining is essentially flat: 19.32 million in 1990 against 19.61 million in 2005,
a rise of 1.47\% over fifteen years, or under 0.1\% a year. The median system
grew 0.48\%, and 47.3\% of systems shrank --- close to an even split. Service-area
systems collectively lost 1.00\% while county-tier systems gained 1.82\%, the
difference being the familiar pattern of small utility footprints losing ground
to their surrounding counties. The mean percentage change of 40.2\% should be
disregarded as a measure of central tendency: it is driven by a handful of very
small systems growing from a near-zero base, of which the most extreme rose from
one resident to 47. The median is the number to quote.

Once the annual roster is respected (Panel B), the residential measure changes
completely. Total exposed residential population fell from 15.13 million in 1990
to 7.30 million in 2005 --- a decline of 51.8\%, roughly halving in fifteen years.
The decomposition shows where it comes from. Of the 340 systems downstream in
1990, 278 were still downstream in 2005 and 62 had left the roster; no system
entered. The 278 stayers gained 1.29\% in population, matching Panel A. The
entire aggregate decline is therefore the extensive margin, driven by systems
leaving the downstream set as upstream mines closed rather than by anyone moving.

That 51.8\% figure should not be read as the headline, however, and
Section~\ref{sec:backcast-step3} explains why. The 62 exiting systems are almost
all county-tier, so the 7.92 million residents they remove from the total is
mostly county population that was never theirs to serve. On the reported-served
measure the same 62 systems account for only about 50,000 people, and exposure
falls by $7.3$\% rather than half. The residential decline is real in direction
but heavily inflated in magnitude by the fallback geography.

\paragraph{The annual series.}
Table \ref{tab:backcast_exposure_series} reports $E^{G}_t$ year by year. Exposure
falls throughout, though not evenly. The steepest early contraction runs from 1991
to 1995 --- 14.57 million down to 10.26 million as ARP Phase I compliance
approached --- with the single largest pre-2005 drop in 1992 ($-15.8$\%). Erosion
continues thereafter, reaching 3.84 million and 245 systems by 2024; the sharpest
fall in the whole series is a $-22.8$\% drop in 2017, well outside the study
window. The count of downstream systems declines monotonically across the entire
period, from 340 to 245, while $E^{G}_t$ itself ticks up in five years when the
surviving systems' population growth outweighed that year's exits.

\paragraph{Relation to the identification strategy.}
These magnitudes are consistent with, but logically independent of, the
instrument. The Acid Rain Program's Phase I deadline in 1995 reduced demand for
high-sulfur coal and hence production in high-sulfur watersheds, and the roster
exits concentrated in the early-to-mid 1990s are what that mechanism predicts for
downstream utility exposure. The backcast is nonetheless a descriptive
accounting: it counts who was downstream of an active mine, and no part of its
construction uses violation or enforcement data, so it can be used to weight the
regression sample without circularity.

\subsubsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The 1990--1997 window is unanchored.} No \texttt{PWSID}-linkable
    reported population exists before SYR2 ($\approx$1998), so $r$ over
    1990--1997 is held flat at its earliest observed value and all movement in
    $\hat S$ comes from the residential backbone. Any pre-1998 trend is therefore
    a Census trend, and the better-anchored 1998--2005 segment should carry more
    weight.
  \item \textbf{County-tier geography drives the residential levels.} 127 of 366
    systems inherit a whole county's population, contributing 84\% of the 1990
    residential total while accounting for a small fraction of reported
    customers. This is why $E^{G}_t$ and $E^{S}_t$ diverge so sharply, and it is
    the single most important caveat on the residential series: its levels, and
    much of its decline, are properties of the fallback geography rather than of
    the utilities.
  \item \textbf{Anchors are inventory values, not annual observations.} Each
    source stamps one population per system, so the anchor panel is short and
    irregular (four possible dates, 1998--2011) and the interpolation between
    them is doing real work. The ratio-drift diagnostic ($1.15\times$ for the
    median service-area system) bounds how much that assumption strains, but does
    not eliminate it.
  \item \textbf{Fixed polygons.} SABS boundaries are contemporary and are held
    fixed back to 1990. Annexation and consolidation over the intervening decades
    mean the 1990 polygon is measured with error; the direction is towards
    overstating early population, since service areas have generally grown.
  \item \textbf{Constant coverage ratio.} Equation \eqref{eq:chain-closed} assumes
    $\kappa$ is fixed within each decade. The assumption is re-imposed every ten
    years rather than over the full window, but the decade-boundary resets reveal
    that some drift occurs.
  \item \textbf{1990 floor.} The 1983--1989 portion of the roster cannot be
    recovered without metro-only 1980 geography, which would select on
    urbanization.
  \item \textbf{Aggregation cuts both ways.} Headcounts are robust because
    idiosyncratic system-level errors cancel in the sum. Any statement about a
    \emph{particular} system is much less reliable than the totals.
\end{enumerate}
