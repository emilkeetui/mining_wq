\begin{center}\begin{abstract}
		\noindent Inducing self-reporting compliance with regulatory standards requires careful incentive compatibility design when self-reporting can be incriminating. I study how US drinking water utilities' self-reporting compliance responds to exogenous decreases in regulated water quality caused by coal mining contamination. I construct utility exposure to water contamination from coal mining with an instrumental variable that uses the plausibly exogenous temporal and spatial changes in coal mining induced by the Acid Rain Program. I find that an additional coal mine upstream of a utility causes a large and significant increase in utility violations for not testing and reporting water quality tests. This is driven by an increase in the cost of testing due to contamination, without a commensurate increase in the penalties for not monitoring. 
\end{abstract}\end{center}

\section{Introduction}

Self reporting is a common monitoring technology for regulation as diverse as personal income and water quality. Self reporters are better able to observe the true level of compliance than regulators when the regulated activity is private. However, regulated entities have disincentives to self report accurately when there are penalties for non-compliance. The regulator can encourage accurate self reporting by using penalties and audit inspections. Self reporting systems are favored by resource constrained regulators since they move the information gathering costs onto the regulated. For the regulated, the costs of self reporting can be large and can discourage self reporting. The amount of self reporting required often relates to the amount of regulated activity being undertaken. For example, drinking water utilities are required to test and report water quality tests to state regulators. The reporting burden of drinking water utilities is higher at more impaired utilities. In this paper I identify the impact of increasing regulated activity on self reporting requirements in the context of drinking water utilities exposed to water contamination from coal mining.

To begin with I identify drinking water utilities' exposure to coal mining using an instrumental variable.


Utilities are responsible for testing and reporting the results of their water quality tests to state regulators. Utilities are the ideal entities to study to isolate the effect of contamination on self-reporting requirements since they do not produce the primary source of water contamination, unlike most entities whose output is self-monitored for environmental quality which simultaneously produce and treat the emissions. 


I construct an instrumental variable for the amount of coal produced upstream of a drinking water utility using the differential impact the acid rain program (ARP) had on coal production with different sulfur content. This IV identifies the amount of coal mining to which a drinking water utility is exposed, which is exogenous to the utility's characteristics and drinking water regulation. The ARP's scope and timing was independent of water quality, local drinking water utility characteristics, and national water legislation.


 

The amount of self reporting required depends on the size of the regulated output, which the regulated entity can often control, presenting a challenge to empirically separate the effect of output changes from the regulated entity's endogenous decisions over output.

Drinking water utilities are unlike those other regulated entities because they are regulated and required to self report on the quality of their water, which is the output after cleaning the pollution produced upstream. There are exceptions: byproducts from disinfectants used to treat water can become harmful contaminants at high enough levels. I study the effect of drinking water utility self reporting from increasing water contamination. In this paper I study the effect of raising water contamination due to coal mining on self reporting of water quality at drinking water utilities.

The extent of the health impacts of water pollution for a large majority of US households are determined by the responses of water utilities, known as community water systems (utility), that provide drinking water to 85 percent of US households. The quality of water supplied by water utilities is regulated by the Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) at the federal level. The SDWA requires utilities to remove contaminants for certain chemicals and pollutants and conduct water quality tests which they submit to state regulators to prove they are meeting water quality standards. Regulators issue a maximum contaminant level (MCL) violation to a utility when the reported concentration of a regulated contaminant exceeds levels safe for human health. MCL violations often trigger costly enforcement responses, such as compliance agreements, consent decrees, and treatment infrastructure upgrades. Regulators cannot issue MCL violations unless utilities self-monitor and self-report their water quality. Monitoring and reporting is a costly requirement in and of itself, requiring the hiring of technical staff to collect samples, laboratories to process samples, and administrative staff to submit test results and follow the testing schedule deadlines. To encourage utilities to self-monitor and self-report water quality tests, regulators issue monitoring and reporting (MR) violations when a utility fails to test or submit tests to the regulator. MR violations are penalized far less harshly, such as with a notice of violation or a public notification requirement. A utility facing rising compliance costs due to upstream pollution has a strategic option: by failing to test or report, it incurs a less costly MR violation while avoiding the detection that would trigger a more costly MCL enforcement response. This paper asks if the regulation of the Safe Drinking Water Act mitigates water contamination.

Utilities are required to remove several inorganic chemical contaminants that can be produced during coal mining through acid mine drainage. Acid mine drainage (AMD) occurs when sulfide-bearing rocks are exposed during mining, oxidize on contact with air and water, produce sulfuric acid, dissolve heavy metals, and leach into surface and groundwater \parencite{skousen2019acid}. Rising IOC pollution increases the costs utilities must spend to remove the contamination and remain compliant with the SDWA. Both privately owned and publicly owned utilities are incentivized to lower costs; private utilities have a profit maximizing incentive, and public utilities are incentivized to avoid raising utility rates to pay rising costs. How utilities respond when pollution removal costs increase due to rising pollution determines the health consequences of coal mining on drinking water. The environmental and health costs of coal will continue into the near future as US and global coal consumption and policy support coal mining and coal fired electricity generation. Coal was used to generate 16 percent of US electricity and one-third of global electricity in 2024 \parencite{IEA2025GER}. US federal energy policy under President Donald Trump has expanded mining access on federal lands and intervened to prevent planned coal fired power plant retirements \parencite{Trump2025EO14260,Trump2025EO14261,Trump2025Proc10914,Daly2025CoalMining}. Pollution from coal mining will primarily affect the communities that live in close proximity to the mines, unlike coal fired electricity generation, which produces global greenhouse gasses along with local air quality pollution. This paper asks whether US coal mining degrades the drinking water quality of downstream communities and whether drinking water regulation protects those communities.

Rising upstream pollution raises the costs of removing contaminants, making it costlier to avoid an MCL violation and increasing the need to test and report water, thereby raising costs spent on monitoring and reporting. The benefits to a utility from not testing and reporting, or from delaying a test or a report, are the cost savings on lab tests, personnel, administrative costs, and avoiding an MCL violation if the tests would reveal a concentration above an MCL. The benefits to a utility from avoiding or delaying an MCL are the cost savings from avoiding costly enforcement, which may require financing and installing new treatment infrastructure or raising rates to pay for their installation. Whether rising compliance costs due to upstream pollution lead utilities to change their compliance with the SDWA is an open empirical question. Utilizing MR violations over MCLs obscures pollution precisely in the cases where it is most severe.

Identifying the causal effect of upstream mining on utility behavior is complicated by two sources of endogeneity. First, coal mines are located in areas with particular geological characteristics, such as coal seam accessibility and transportation corridors, that may also independently predict water quality and utility characteristics. This may understate the true effect of coal mining on utility violations and water quality. Second, utilities with more political influence may successfully resist upstream mine siting, and those same systems may be better at maintaining compliance, generating a negative correlation between upstream mining and utility ability that understates the true compliance burden mining imposes. This leads to OLS underestimating the effect of coal mines on utility violations.

I address these identification challenges by instrumenting for the location and production of coal mines using the Acid Rain Program (ARP), enacted as part of the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990. The ARP required large coal-fired power plants, the dominant consumers of coal, accounting for approximately 90 percent of US coal demand, to reduce their sulfur dioxide emissions, with compliance for Phase I of the ARP mandatory by 1995. The least costly way for most plants to comply was to switch from high-sulfur coal to low-sulfur coal with less than two percent sulfur content by weight. This created a sharp, nationally-determined shift in coal demand: after 1995, demand for high-sulfur coal fell substantially, while demand for low-sulfur coal increased. As a result, coal production declined in high-sulfur mining areas and expanded in low-sulfur areas. Our instrument interacts a post-1995 indicator with the average sulfur content of coal deposits in the watershed directly upstream of each utility's water intake. This interaction predicts changes in upstream mine activity driven by the ARP's differential demand shock across areas with different coal sulfur content and is orthogonal to the unobserved characteristics of utilities that determine compliance behavior. The ARP was a national air quality regulation drafted independently of any consideration of drinking water quality; its timing and design were determined to address acid rain and electricity generator compliance timelines and were unrelated to local utility characteristics, SDWA regulatory changes, or the ability of any utility to manage local water contamination.

I construct a panel from 1985 to 2005 linking utilities to annual coal production occurring in the watershed upstream of their water intakes. Mine locations come from the Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA); annual mine-level production from the Energy Information Administration (EIA); watershed coal sulfur content from USGS COALQUAL; watershed connectivity from USGS NHDplus; and utility violation and enforcement records from the EPA's Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS). I supplement these data with measured contaminant concentrations from the EPA's second Six-Year Review (SYR2), which collected compliance monitoring readings at utilities from 1998 to 2005. This concentration dataset allows us to directly verify that upstream coal production raises inorganic chemical contamination at utilities independent of violation behavior.

I find that coal mining raises utility inorganic chemical (IOC) monitoring and reporting (MR) violations, failures to conduct and report required water quality tests. An additional active coal mine one watershed upstream of a utility's water intake increases the number of days per year a utility spends in violation of the SDWA by approximately 14 to 20 days, depending on the IOC contaminant. The 2SLS coefficients on MCL violations are statistically and economically insignificant for all contaminants, with point estimates an order of magnitude below those for MR violations. Coal mining significantly raises the incidence of non-monitoring but has no effect on increasing detected contaminant exceedances above contaminant limits.

The increase in MR violations is driven by an increase in contaminant concentration, which increases the benefit of non-compliance. Higher levels of pollution raise the benefit of non-compliance: an MCL violation is more likely at higher levels of pollution, so avoiding testing avoids potentially high penalties. Treatment costs more as pollution levels rise, increasing the cost savings from every level of negligence. Upstream coal mining measurably raises actual IOC concentrations in utility water. Every additional 10 million short tons of coal mined upstream since 1985 increases mean arsenic concentrations by 0.0023 mg/L, approximately 100 percent of the average arsenic concentration level in the sample, as well as chromium by 50 percent, selenium by 69 percent, and barium by 24 percent of their respective sample means. These effects are economically and statistically significant. The concentrations measured in the SYR2 data are drawn from water tests that were conducted and reported, but the majority of SDWA violations in our sample are issued precisely for failing to conduct such tests. The true contamination distribution during MR violation periods is unobserved by regulators and researchers alike, and the rising concentration evidence suggests the worst contamination is concentrated during the periods of non-monitoring.

The increase in MR violations downstream of coal mining is also driven by a reduction in enforcement penalties. An additional upstream mine reduces the probability that a utility receives formal enforcement action, the more costly regulatory response relative to informal enforcement action, by 5.6 percentage points, while increasing the probability of a sanitary visit by 14 percentage points. Regulators substitute less punitive responses as upstream pollution rises. This is consistent with a regulatory system in which the social cost of formal enforcement, including the potential system disruptions and capital expenditures it imposes, leads regulators to exercise discretion toward lenient responses when the compliance burden on utilities is high, but it reinforces rather than offsets the non-compliance behavior of utilities facing rising contamination costs.

The rising benefits of non-compliance and the falling penalties for non-compliance reinforce each other to increase SDWA non-compliance with upstream coal mining. Adapting the regulatory compliance framework of \textcite{kang2021understanding}, I show that utilities choose higher negligence levels, conduct fewer tests, and submit fewer reports to regulators when compliance costs rise and when penalties fall. Our empirical results confirm both: mining raises contamination and thus increases the cost of maintaining compliance; and it also triggers softer enforcement responses that reduce the expected penalty from non-compliance.

These results contribute to three strands of the literature. The first concerns the environmental consequences of coal mining and natural resource extraction. Existing work documents coal mining's effects on air quality, greenhouse gas emissions, and health \parencite{muller2011environmental,parfitt2024there}, and finds evidence of water quality degradation from mining in international settings \parencite{aragon2016polluting,chu2023air}. Our paper provides the first causal estimates of coal mining's downstream effect on the drinking water quality of treated water at utilities in the United States, identifying that coal mining pollution crosses the gap from environmental contamination to piped household water.

The second contribution is to the literature on strategic environmental self-monitoring and reporting. Firms regulated by environmental standards face incentives to under-report emissions when the penalties for revelation exceed the penalties for non-disclosure \parencite{duflo2013truth,duflo2018value,mu2024s,helland1998enforcement}. Utilities are a distinctive setting: unlike the firms in this literature, they are not the source of the pollution they are regulated to remove. Their incentive to undermonitor arises not from hiding their own emissions but from avoiding the detection of upstream contamination that would require costly remediation. This generates a clean identification opportunity: the ARP-driven change in upstream coal mining is plainly exogenous to the utility's own historical reporting behavior, ruling out the reverse causality concerns that complicate identification in contexts where the firm controls both the pollution and its measurement. I provide direct evidence that MR violations rise alongside measured contamination increases, strengthening the causal inference that non-monitoring is strategic rather than accidental. Prior work on utility strategic violations has relied on violations data alone \parencite{wallsten2008effects,foster2019arsenic}. The joint observation of rising contaminant concentrations, rising MR violations, and falling formal enforcement, all causally attributed to the same upstream production shock, provides substantially stronger evidence of strategic compliance behavior than prior studies.

The third contribution is to the literature on US drinking water quality and the SDWA. Water quality in the US has improved substantially since the passage of the Clean Water Act and SDWA \parencite{keiser2019consequences}; yet between 9 and 34 million people per year are served by utilities in violation of health-based standards \parencite{allaire2018national}. Our findings suggest that the regulatory design of the SDWA, which relies on utility self-monitoring and assigns substantially lower penalties to non-monitoring than to detected contamination, creates systematic incentives to undertest in precisely the circumstances where testing matters most. Strengthening the monitoring schedule or increasing penalties for non-monitoring in high-pollution areas may be a more effective lever for improving drinking water outcomes than increases in MCL stringency alone.

\section{Institutional Background}

\subsection{Drinking water utilities, regulators, and the Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA)}

I focus on drinking water utilities downstream of coal mines between 1985 and 2005. In particular, I focus on a class of drinking water utilities known legally as community water systems that serve at least 25 consumers through at least 15 connections throughout the year, since these are regulated by the Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA). Utilities are generally not the primary source of water contamination and must instead treat contamination generated upstream. The SDWA limits the concentrations of contaminants in utility water by setting maximum contaminant levels (MCL) and requiring utilities to self report their compliance with MCLs.

Utilities that exceed an MCL for a specific contaminant receive a contaminant specific violation. Utilities that fail to self report water quality are not presumed to be in violation of an MCL but only of failing to meet a monitoring requirement. An MCL violation can be hidden from the regulator as long as the test for that contaminant is not self-reported by the utility. Utilities receive a violation for failing to meet the monitoring requirement schedule, known as a monitoring and reporting (MR) violation. The testing and reporting schedule varied over time and by contaminant risk. The largest change to testing schedules occurred in 1991 when the testing schedule for contaminants was standardized nationally to occur on three year compliance cycles. Systems had to re-test for contaminants every three years to demonstrate the contaminant was not present in the system. The number of tests required varied by regulator assessed risk of that contaminant to the water supply. 

MCL violations are serious threats to public health and require timely rectification. MR violations are not treated as immediate threats to public health and receive less costly enforcement action than MCL violations. MR violations receive less costly enforcement than MCL violations of the same contaminant.

Utilities found monitoring and reporting requirements to be difficult to meet due to the administrative and technical burden. Utilities needed to be administratively organized to conduct the right tests and report them on schedule. Technical staff were required to conduct separate tests for contaminants or to prepare samples to be sent to laboratories. Indiana drinking water utility managers who responded to a 1980 survey on the impact of the SDWA said that the SDWA's reporting requirements had the greatest impact on their systems, while MCLs had the least \parencite{oleckno1982national}.

The list of regulated contaminants was added to over time; in 1985 there were 23 regulated contaminants and by 2005 there were 93. I focus on arsenic, nitrates, barium, and selenium because their MCLs did not change between 1985 and 2005 and they are water contaminants that can be generated by coal mining. Environmental pollution from coal mining can include heavy metals, radioactive elements, non-organic compound pollutants, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in the environment \parencite{rouhani2023overview,boente2020benzo,liang2017spatial}. Inorganic chemical pollution is commonly produced during coal mining through acid mine drainage. Acid mine drainage occurs when rocks containing sulfides are exposed to air and water, beginning the process of oxidation and decomposition \parencite{ceto2000abandoned}. The oxidized minerals form sulfuric acid, which dissolves metal ions and sulfates that can then be carried into surface or groundwater \parencite{akcil2006acid,skousen2019acid}.

The SDWA is enforced by the states, which are responsible for bringing violating utilities into SDWA compliance and have wide discretionary power to decide what enforcement is appropriate. Except for a subset of violating circumstances and mandatory notifications to consumers that utilities must provide when in violation, the SDWA gives states wide discretionary power to tailor compliance and enforcement plans to each utility and violation, with the intention that the violation is resolved quickly enough to reduce the health impacts of contamination. 

Enforcement is frequently remedial rather than punitive in addressing the cause of the violation. Enforcement can be informal (letters, notices of violation, compliance plans), formal (court mandated compliance plans, legal prosecution, fines), or involve visits (sanitary, sampling, technical assistance) to the utility. Non-fine enforcement is still costly; compliance plans can require installing treatment infrastructure or hiring technical personnel, and public notifications for violations have large reputational costs that deter violations \parencite{bennear2008impacts}.

\section{Theoretical model}

In the model, utilities find it costly to meet regulatory standards and have an incentive to reduce effort following \textcite{mookherjee1994marginal}. Regulators use penalties and investigations for not self reporting and exceeding regulatory standards for water quality. Penalties differ if a utility fails to self report and exceeds a regulatory standard compared to if a utility fails to self report and does not exceed a regulatory standard following \textcite{kaplow1994optimal}.

\subsection{Setup}

The utility chooses a negligence level $a \in [0, \bar{a}]$, which measures how much they avoid the requirements of drinking water quality regulations, from avoiding every requirement $\bar{a}$ to full compliance 0. Failing to conduct or submit tests to the regulator or not installing pollution abatement technology are examples of utility negligence. Regulators do not directly observe utility choice of $a$. Utilities face a compliance cost, $\theta$, that is a random variable drawn from $\Theta$, which is a strictly increasing and continuously differentiable function $F(\cdot)$ with support $(0, \bar{\theta})$. Utilities know their draw of $\theta$, but regulators only know the distribution of $\theta$. A compliance cost is determined by both a utilityâ€™s ability to manage a drinking water system, such as the skills of managerial and technical staff, and by the costs of water treatment, including the cost of filtration equipment or sterilization chemicals. Utilities receive a benefit $\theta b(a)$ from having compliance costs $\theta$ and choosing a negligence level $a$. A utility's benefits from not conducting a test for a specific contaminant are the wages and lab fees for sampling that it saved. The opportunity cost to the utility of choosing a compliance level $a$ relative to maximum negligence $\bar{a}$ is $\theta[b\bar{a}-b(a)]$. The financial benefit of a certain level of negligence to a profit maximizing privately owned utility is obvious. Publicly owned utilities also benefit from cost savings that allow them to forgo rate increases or tax collection, which may be unpopular with residents.

Utilities face $N$ required monitoring events per year. Given negligence $a$, each sampling event would reveal an MCL exceedance with probability $q(a)$, with $q(0)=0$, $q'>0$, and $q'' \ge 0$.

%(The number of true exceedances is then Binomial $(N, q(a)) \approx$ Poisson with mean $Nq(a)$, so Section 1's Poisson structure is recovered with intensity $Nq(a)$ in place of $a$.) For the empirically relevant range, $q(a)$ is small: mining degrades source water \emph{within} the MCL for most systems.

Self reporting costs $c$ are utility-specific and depend on upstream contamination $m$ so that $c \sim G(\cdot\,;m)$, with $m$ shifting the distribution rightward (first-order stochastic dominance). Degraded source water triggers additional required sampling even when contamination remains below the MCL.


Utilities receive sanction $r$ if they are above the MCL and they self report. Regulators investigate utilities that do not report with probability $p$ and those utilities they investigate receive a sanction $t$. Utilities receive penalty $s$ if they are investigated and are above the MCL, $s>r$.

%the sanctions $(r,s,t)$ and enforcement intensity $p$ do not depend on $m$.

For each required self reporting event the utility chooses to self report (cost $c$) or not (an MR violation). Per-event expected penalties:
\begin{align*}
\text{report:} \quad & c + q(a)\,r \\
\text{skip:} \quad & p\left[t + q(a)\,s\right]
\end{align*}
Taking $r > ps$ throughout, the utility self reports iff
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chat}
c \le \hat{c}(a) \equiv pt - (r - ps)\,q(a).
\end{equation}
Even a system with no MCL violations, $q(a) = 0$, stops self reporting once $c > pt$, the self reporting costs more than the expected sanction for skipping it, and no exceedance exists to hide. The self reporting threshold is decreasing in $q(a)$, so systems closer to (or above) the MCL abandon self reporting at lower cost thresholds. The expected penalty is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endog_penalty}
e(a) = N \min\left\{\, c + r\,q(a),\;\; pt + ps\,q(a) \,\right\},
\end{equation}
which is increasing in $a$.

\subsection{Optimal negligence}

The utility maximizes $-\theta[b(\bar{a}) - b(a)] - e(a)$. The first-order condition is piecewise:
\begin{align}
\theta b'(a) &= N r\, q'(a) & \text{(testing region, } c \le \hat{c}(a)) \\
\theta b'(a) &= N ps\, q'(a) & \text{(MR region, } c > \hat{c}(a))
\end{align}
Because $ps < r$, the marginal penalty is lower when the utility is in the MR region than when it is self reporting. When $c$ increases exogenously the region of MR increases. Suppose costs increase such that $c_1 =\hat{c}(a_1) < c_2$. The increase in $c$ pushes a system from self reporting into the MR region, its marginal penalty falls from $Nr\,q'(a)$ to $Nps\,q'(a)$ and since $b'(a)<0$, its negligence increases.

\subsection{The propositions}

The model predicts that when water quality deteriorates due to increased contamination, self reporting rates fall.

\textbf{Proposition 1:} $\partial a/\partial\theta = b'(a) / [\,-\theta b''(a) + N\rho\, q''(a)\,] > 0$ within each region (where $\rho \in \{r, ps\}$ is the region's sanction), with an upward jump at $\hat{\theta}$. Negligence is globally increasing in $\theta$.

\textbf{Proposition 2}: Contamination from mining creates ambiguous effects on MR violation rates, $\partial MR/\partial m$.
Mining contamination acts on self reporting through the cost of self reporting and the penalties for exceeding the MCL. On the cost side, $c$ is drawn from $G(\cdot;m)$ with $m$ shifting the distribution rightward (first-order stochastic dominance), so that each self reporting cost draw is higher. The area of the MR violation in the first order condition increases. If self reporting costs increase by a large enough amount, this can result in MR violations from self reporting costs outweighing penalties, even for systems with no MCL violation, $q(0)$.
Direct contamination from mining inducing an MCL has an ambiguous effect on self reporting. From Equation \ref{eq:chat}, contamination will increase MR violations if the expected penalty from being in violation and not reporting ($psq(a)$) outweighs the penalty from being in MCL violation and self reporting ($rq(a)$). If investigations are unlikely or ineffective in discovering MCL violations, then utilities have an incentive not to self report when contamination rises.

The identifying content of the cost channel is that $G$ is the distribution of the cost of executing and reporting a compliant test, $c$, which is a different object from the contaminant concentration that drives $q(a)$ (the probability a sample exceeds an MCL). Mining can shift $G$ rightward while leaving $q$ essentially unchanged. The concentration stays below the MCL, so $q$, and hence recorded MCL violations, do not move, yet running a compliant monitoring program becomes more expensive:
More constituents to sample. Mining-adjacent source water carries elevated contaminants which even at sub-MCL levels are high enough that more tests are needed to be conducted. Detecting any elevation, even below an MCL, can push a system into a higher monitoring frequency or additional source-water points, raising $N$ and hence total testing cost $N\cdot c$.

\textbf{Proposition 3:} scaling $(r,s,t) \to \lambda(r,s,t)$ scales $e'(a)$ by $\lambda$ in both regions, so $a$ falls; and $\hat{c}(a) = \lambda\left[pt - (r-ps)q(a)\right]$ scales up by $\lambda$ while $c$ does not, so the testing region expands. Higher penalties reduce negligence and increase self reporting.

\section{Data}

\subsection{Utility characteristic, regulator, and violation data}

The EPA's Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS) Enforcement and Compliance History Online (ECHO) data contains the universe of drinking water utilities that were active continuously between 1985 and 2005. I form an annual panel of utilities that were active between 1985 and 2005, had a water intake located in a watershed directly downstream from at least one active coal mine during at least one year between 1985 and 2005, had water intakes with data on sulfur as a percentage of coal weight, and had no water intakes in a watershed with a coal mine that was active at any time between 1985 and 2005. As I explain later in the data section, this rule for utility water intakes ensures that I identify utilities with impaired water intakes. My sample contains 340 unique utilities. 

I construct a panel of utilities active between 1985 and 2005 using the SDWIS ECHO variables containing the year of activation and the year of deactivation. Time varying utility characteristic data is limited during this time period. The only time varying characteristic I use is the number of active facilities, such as water intake and treatment plants that a utility operates. I create this from the date a facility was constructed and the date it was deactivated. 

I use SDWIS ECHO data to construct outcome variables for MR and MCL violations for a general inorganic chemical (IOC) contaminant rule, the arsenic rule, and the nitrate rule. I exclude any IOC violations that occur at the same time and at the same utility as an arsenic or nitrate violation to avoid double counting. SDWA uniquely identifies utility violations with information on the start and end dates of the violation, the type of SDWA rule violated, and the contaminant. Arsenic and nitrate MCLs did not change during this period, and they are contaminants that may be related to coal mining. The Inorganic chemical rule contains all IOCs during this period, some of which are subject to changing MCLs. Nitrate and arsenic MR requirements changed during this period; however, they changed nationally as part of the three year compliance reporting schedule. Year fixed effects in empirical models address some of the concerns that changes in MR violations would be the result of changing MR requirements. 

The primary outcome variable for utility violations is a binary variable equal to one if the utility experienced a violation of that type anytime during the year. The dates of the violation in the data do not necessarily reflect that the utility was continuously violating during that period. Violations are often triggered when utilities fail to meet testing schedules and then continue for a set period of time after they are issued. Figure \ref{img:mineviolengthhist_downstream} plots histograms of the lengths of arsenic, IOC, and nitrate violations. There is a tendency for the length of time of violations to align with yearly quarters. As a robustness test, I measure violations as the days out of a year in violation rather than as a binary measure of whether the violation took place during any part of the year.

Table \ref{tab:ioc_days_dwnstrm} presents means and standard deviations of the number of days out of a year that a utility spends with an IOC MR and MCL violation. On average, a utility spends between 10 and 15 days out of a year with an MR violation and less than a day with an MCL violation. This reflects that MCL violations are health dangers and are resolved as quickly as possible, as well as the rarity of MCL violations, where, in a given year, most utilities do not receive an MCL violation. The large standard deviation of 56 to 67 days reflects the multi-modal distribution that characterizes the number of days a utility is in violation: many utilities do not receive an MR violation in a year, and those that do tend to experience it for multiple quarters of the year.

\subsection{Utility Contaminant Measurement Data}

I measure contaminant concentrations at utilities with annual mean contaminant concentrations of several inorganic chemical contaminants in raw and treated utility water using the second Six-Year Review data (SYR2, sample years 1998-2005). 

Starting in 1991, the USEPA began conducting reviews of all National Primary Drinking Water Regulations every six years to determine whether the rules needed revisions. The first SYR1 has not been made publicly available. The SYR2 was the second round of the six year review covering 1998 to 2005. The SYR2 data contained contaminant data for 89 percent of the total population served by a drinking water system. EPA collected SYR2 data by requesting states to submit their utility SDWA compliance data. Unrelated to the SYR and to ensure compliance with the SDWA, states were required to collect compliance monitoring data from utilities but were not required to submit that data to the USEPA alongside the SDWA violation reports.  Pennsylvania, Mississippi, Kansas, and Louisiana were not included in the SYR2 partly due to a lack of a national database that received and stored the occurrence of contaminants at utilities at the time.

I clean the SYR2 data following the procedure of \textcite{ravalli2022sociodemographic}. I retain records from utilities and drop transient and non-transient non-community systems. This removes roughly 23 percent of raw monitoring records. For each contaminant, I compute the national share of records above the maximum laboratory detectable level (MDL), the threshold that a contaminant must be present in a sample to be detected at a laboratory, and retain only contaminants detected in more than 10 percent of records. I replace the observations below the MDL of any contaminant with the MDL/$\sqrt{2}$. Records reporting a concentration greater than 100 times the contaminant's MCL are dropped as data-entry errors. Among the IOCs that can be produced during coal mining, this keeps arsenic, nitrate, barium, and selenium. All mass-concentration readings are converted to mg/L before averaging.

After cleaning, sample-level records are collapsed to the utility\,$\times$\,contaminant\,$\times$\,year level. The annual concentration of a particular contaminant is the mean of that year's samples at a particular utility.

\subsection{River and stream connectivity data}

Drinking water utilities are exposed to water pollution from coal mining when the locations where they source their water are downstream of coal mines. The exposure is strongest when a utility draws from surface water. Groundwater sources downstream of coal mining are also exposed to upstream pollution since groundwater is also recharged by water flow from above. In order to measure which utilities are downstream of coal mining, I identify which watersheds utilities source water from using the NHDplus hydrological connectivity dataset that identifies the boundaries of watersheds across the US and an EPA dataset on the watershed level location of utility water intakes. The watershed dataset is a connected graph that identifies which watersheds flow into one another, allowing me to identify utilities' water source watersheds and any upstream watersheds that flow into a source water watershed. The NHDplus identifies watersheds as geographical boundaries where water that falls within that boundary would drain into the same stream segment. The NHDplus assigns watersheds a unique 2-12 digit code known as a HUC (hydrological unit code). The number of digits corresponds to the size of the area, with the geographical area decreasing with the number of digits; for example, the lower 48 US states are divided into 18 HUC02s and over 87,000 HUC12s (see Figure \ref{img:WBD_Base_HUStructure_small}). I use HUC12 watersheds to identify utility water intakes. HUC12 watersheds are the smallest mapped watersheds available for the entire continental US and are small enough that coal mining activities may impact the water in the HUC12 directly downstream. However, HUC12s are also large enough that activity that takes place where the HUC12 drains into another HUC12 may not affect water quality at a point upstream within the same HUC12. I consider a utility to be downstream of a coal mine if any of its water intakes are within a HUC12 directly downstream of HUC12 where mining took place between 1985 and 2005 and no water intakes are within a HUC12 where coal mining took place between 1985 and 2005.

\section{Identification Strategy}

The locations of utility water intakes and coal mines are non-randomly located with respect to each other. For example, coal mines are sited where they can pass environmental reviews, and a utility downstream of a coal mine will increase the chances of failing an environmental review. A utility with political power may be able to influence regulators to prevent polluting industries from siting upstream, and those systems are also more capable of avoiding violations, leading to a positive correlation between utility SDWA violations and upstream coal mining and omitted variable bias in an OLS estimate of the impact of coal mines on utility drinking water quality violations. This would create a positive bias on the coefficient of the number of mines in a regression on utility violations since an increase in the number of mines is associated with less capable utilities that have more violations.

To estimate the effect of the level of contamination on self reporting, I construct an instrumental variable for coal mining. The IV variable is independent of the utility's ability to meet the water quality self reporting requirements of the regulation. The instrument predicts the location and amount of coal mining occurring upstream of a utility using the amount of sulfur in the coal bed and the shift in coal demand preferences for the percentage of sulfur in coal that occurred after the acid rain program.

\subsection{The Acid Rain Program (ARP) and Coal Mining}

The ARP was signed on November 15, 1990, as an amendment to the Clean Air Act with the intention of reducing acid rain by limiting emissions of acid rain precursors, sulfur dioxide (SO2), and nitrogen oxides (NOx) from coal fired power plants. The regulation capped total national SO2 and NOx emissions. Power plants were given emission allowances for which they were fined if they exceeded the limit. Allowances could be traded on a market, allowing emission efficient plants to sell their allowances and emission inefficient plants to buy allowances. Although compliance could be achieved with SO2 and NOx scrubbers, fuel switching to low sulfur coal was a primary abatement response. The ARP achieved 100 percent compliance and had the intended effect of reducing acid rain \parencite{lattanzio2022clean}. 

Emissions were capped in two phases. In phase I, the EPA issued emission permits and allowances to 110 high polluting, mostly coal-fired electricity generating facilities. Facilities targeted under phase I were required to achieve compliance with SO2 emission reductions by 1995. Phase II included smaller power generating units that used oil, coal, and gas. The deadline for compliance with phase II was January 1, 2000. I focus on the phase I deadline because it targeted the largest coal fired power plants.

Fuel switching to low sulfur coal was a primary method by which electricity generators met the ARP requirements. Since the electric power industry accounted for 90 percent of coal demand in 2000 \parencite{freme2000us}, the ARP increased the demand for low sulfur coal and reduced the demand for high sulfur coal \parencite{epaarpcoalemp,stratford2015coalprod}. Coal's sulfur content varies across mines within and across coal regions. The amount of sulfur present within a coal mine affected its production after 1995.

\subsection{Research Design}

I measure the effect of upstream coal mining on utility self reporting compliance with the SDWA using an instrumental variable regression of SDWA violations on the number of active coal mines upstream of the utility water intake. The number of active coal mines in the HUC12 upstream of a utility water intake is instrumented by interacting a binary variable equal to one during the time period after 1995 and zero otherwise, the compliance date for stage I of the ARP, with the percent of sulfur by weight in the coal deposits within the HUC12 directly upstream of the utility intake. Production in low sulfur coal areas increased to meet the shifting national demand. The instrument exploits the shift in coal fired power plants' demand for coal with lower sulfur after 1995 in order to meet the sulfur dioxide emission standards of the ARP to identify variation in the location and size of coal production that is exogenous to unobserved utility characteristics affecting violations. 

The first stage specification of our instrumental variable regression of the effect of upstream coal mining on utility drinking water violations is

\begin{equation}\label{1ststage2sls}
\begin{split}
\text{NumMines}_{ct} &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1\text{NumIntakes}_{ct} + \alpha_2\text{UpstreamCoalSulfur\%}_c \cdot \text{Post95}_t \\
&\quad + Y_t + C_c + \nu_{ct}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Where the Number of Mines that are one HUC12 upstream of utility c in year t is $NumMines_{ct}$. The exogenous covariates are the number of utility intake facilities, $NumIntakes_{ct}$, year fixed effects, $Y_t$, and utility fixed effects, $C_c$. The instrumental variable is the interaction between the average sulfur as a percentage of weight in the HUC12s directly upstream of all utility c's water intakes, $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c$, and a dummy variable equal to 1 for the years after the deadline to implement stage I of the ARP, 1995, and 0 at other times, $Post95_t$. During the pre-1995 period, all observations of $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t$ are 0, and after 1995, the observations are the percent of sulfur in upstream coal. Standard errors are clustered at the utility level. 

The second stage of the regression is

\begin{equation}\label{2ndstage2sls}
\begin{split}
\text{Days in Violation}_{vct} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1\text{NumIntakes}_{ct} + \gamma\widehat{\text{NumMines}}_{ct} \\
&\quad + Y_t + C_c + \epsilon_{ct}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

In the second stage, the dependent variable is the number of days in a year that utility c experienced violation v in year t, $\text{Days in Violation}_{vct}$. 

Acid mine drainage is more common when the minerals contain high levels of sulfur, making the incidence of acid mine drainage increase with coal sulfur levels \parencite{schweinfurth2009introduction}. The endogeneity of upstream sulfur is controlled for with utility fixed effects since sulfur levels do not change over time.

\subsection{Instrument validity}

The instrument is independent if the relationship between utility characteristics important for water quality and the siting of upstream coal mines is independent of the upstream sulfur coal sulfur content in 1995, so that $f(UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t, \text{utility characteristic})=f(UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t)f(\text{utility characteristic})$. Conditional on the control variables, $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%\times Post95$ is independent of the omitted variables in $\epsilon_{ct}$ that determine utility drinking water quality violations. The ARP and the timing of the first phase were both exogenous to utility drinking water quality and utility characteristics. The ARP was a national clean air policy that was not written or enacted to address water quality issues. The effects of the ARP on coal production were determined by national and international energy markets that local utilities or local utility water quality did not affect. Local utility characteristics regarding water quality or local power over source protection did not affect the scope of the ARP, nor how stringent the ARP law was, nor the degree to which low sulfur coal should be part of the abatement technology, nor the date of phase I of the ARP. The timing of the ARP did not coincide with major drinking water regulations. 

The instrument would violate independence if the unobserved utility characteristics that affect drinking water quality violations and the siting of coal mines changed after 1995 for utilities with different amounts of sulfur as a percentage of coal weight. Knowing about sulfur as a percentage of coal weight upstream of a utility after 1995 tells us nothing about how much political power a local utility has over mine siting. Thus, $Cov(\text{political power of utility}_c, UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t)=0$. 

The instrument only affects utilities through its effect on the number of coal mines. A threat to this would be if utility SDWA regulation changed after 1995 and had a differential impact on utilities downstream of high sulfur coal before and after 1995 compared to utilities downstream of low sulfur coal before and after 1995. Changes in SDWA rules that affected all utilities the same way are controlled for with time fixed effects. For example, in 1991, the monitoring and reporting schedule changed for all utilities and is controlled for with time fixed effects. Any change to an MCL would apply to all utilities and be controlled for with time fixed effects. The SDWA amendments that were passed in 1996 increased the use of consumer confidence reports as an enforcement tool when a utility violated a drinking water regulation. Utilities downstream of high sulfur coal will experience less upstream coal mining after 1995 compared to before 1995, and therefore less upstream water contamination, whereas utilities downstream of low sulfur coal will experience more upstream coal mining after 1995 compared to before 1995. Utilities downstream of high sulfur coal may experience fewer SDWA violations from upstream pollution compared to before 1995, meaning the burden of a consumer confidence report is smaller than it would have been before 1995, whereas utilities downstream of low sulfur coal will experience more upstream pollution after 1995 compared to before, meaning the burden of the consumer confidence report is higher than it would have been before 1995. This difference is likely small since all utilities in the sample are downstream of coal mining and have more similar pollution exposure and burden from consumer confidence reports compared to the average utility in the country.

I can check this by looking at the relationships between ARPxpost95 and utility characteristics not associated with the number of upstream mines.

The instrument does predict the location and size of coal production. Figure \ref{fig:scatterhuccoalsulfur} is a scatter plot of the number of coal mines and the coal sulfur percent in the HUC12s upstream of utilities before and after 1995, with plotted linear lines of best fit for each period. The change in production away from high sulfur coal production after the ARP is strongly demonstrated by the line of best fit flattening after 1995, indicating that while there was more coal production in higher sulfur coal areas before 1995, there was a large shift to areas with low sulfur coal after 1995. The number of coal mines in a HUC12 increased with the percent of sulfur as the weight of coal in the HUC12 before 1995. The time contingent switch in coal production based on sulfur content supports the structure of the variable $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t$. 

The strength of the instrument is still evident after controlling for covariates. \subsubsection{Assessing monotonicity}

The instrument requires monotonicity and for there to be no defiers to recover the local average treatment effect (LATE) estimate of the instrument. A defier is a utility that is upstream of a high-sulfur coal watershed where coal production increased after 1995 but would have decreased production after 1995 if it had instead been a low-sulfur coal watershed. A never-taker is a high-HUC12 that maintains production after 1995 because its customers are electricity generators that install scrubbers. As long as scrubber adoption is not positively correlated with sulfur in a way that causes production to increase post-ARP in high-sulfur areas, this is not a violation of monotonicity and only reduces first-stage power. To be a defier with scrubber installation requires a power plant to expand production after installing scrubbers and increase electricity market share. Power plants that rely on high sulfur coal and scrubbers are more likely to lose market share and reduce production since scrubbers are expensive to install, increase operating costs, and require higher electricity rates to retain profits. If some high-sulfur regions had a comparative advantage in low-cost production that offset the ARP cost increase, such as very low labor costs or transportation advantages, they might expand production even as ARP made their coal relatively more expensive. Unless this expansion would not have occurred if the low cost high sulfur regions had been low sulfur coal regions, this would not create defiers and would instead create never-takers.

Stating monotonicity more formally and following \textcite{angrist1994identification}, let $\Psi$ be the support of the instrument $Z_i$, and for each $z \in \Psi$ define a random variable $D_i(z)$. The monotonicity assumption requires that for all $z, w \in \Psi$, either $D_i(z) \leq D_i(w)$ for all $i$, or $D_i(z) \geq D_i(w)$ for all $i$. This condition applies pairwise to elements of $\Psi$ and imposes no restriction on the ordering of $z$ and $w$. In this setting, the instrument is $Z_i = \text{post95}_t \times \text{sulfur}_h$, which equals zero before 1995 and $\text{sulfur}_h$ after. For a given sulfur level $w$, monotonicity requires either $\text{num_mines}_i(0) \leq \text{num_mines}_i(w)$ for all $i$, or $\text{num_mines}_i(0) \geq \text{num_mines}_i(w)$ for all $i$. This permits the direction of the response to vary with sulfur content. For example, at $\text{sulfur} = 1$ (low sulfur), mines may expand after 1995, so monotonicity requires $\text{num_mines}_i(0) \leq \text{num_mines}_i(1)$ for all $i$. At $\text{sulfur} = 4$ (high sulfur), mines likely contract after 1995, so monotonicity requires $\text{num_mines}_i(0) \geq \text{num_mines}_i(4)$ for all $i$. These two conditions are compatible: monotonicity is satisfied so long as each pairwise comparison is uniform across units, even when low- and high-sulfur areas respond in opposite directions.

\section{The impact of coal mining on utility self reporting}

Table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_minevio_allcat_ivsum_binvio} reports the results of Equation \ref{2ndstage2sls} estimating the impact of the number of active upstream coal mines on the likelihood that in a year a utility receives either an MCL or MR violation for nitrates, arsenic, and inorganic chemicals using OLS, reduced form, and two stage least squares regressions. 

An additional active coal mine upstream increases the likelihood of nitrate, arsenic, and IOC SDWA violations by roughly 2 percentage points days out of the year when using OLS. The effect is statistically significant at all levels.

Coal production fell most acutely in high sulfur coal areas after 1995, and a positive relationship between coal production and SDWA violations predicts that IOC violations would fall with increasing upstream sulfur after 1995 relative to before 1995. The reduced form effect of the instrument measures how much more (or less) a one-percentage-point increase in upstream sulfur affects the likelihood of violations after ARP Phase I relative to before. An additional percent of coal sulfur upstream leads to a 2.4 percentage point reduction in the likelihood a utility experiences a nitrate, arsenic, or inorganic chemical violation of any kind after 1995 compared to before 1995. This suggests that in areas where the number of coal mines is more likely to decrease, the likelihood of violation in a year falls.

The instrumental variable estimate of an additional active coal mine upstream on nitrates and arsenic violations is an additional 10 and 7.5 percentage point increases, respectively. The instrument has a relatively strong first stage with an F statistic of 27.5. The OLS results underestimate the effect of coal mines on violations relative to the IV results. In general, the OLS impact of coal mines is five times smaller than the IV coefficient, suggesting that the omitted variable bias is negative. A negative omitted variable bias is consistent with utilities that are able to influence the siting of upstream mines being better at avoiding violations.

The entire increase in violations from upstream mining is driven by MR violations rather than MCL violations. The regressions presented in Table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_minevio_mr_ivsum_binvio} estimate Equation \ref{2ndstage2sls} on MR arsenic, nitrate, and IOC violations. An additional mine upstream of a utility leads to 9, 7.4, and 7 percentage point increases in the likelihood of an MR violation in a year for nitrates, arsenic, and general IOC contaminants, respectively, comprising the entire effect observed when the dependent variable combines MR violations with MCL violations. IOC violations are statistically significant at the 10 percent level when violations are limited to MR violations and statistically insignificant when the violations refer to the share of the year spent in either MCL or MR violations. Violations are more than twice as likely to occur relative to the average rate of MR violations after an additional mine operates upstream. Table \ref{tab:mr_mcl_incidence_summary} reports the mean summary rates of arsenic, nitrate, and inorganic chemical violations at 4 percent, 6 percent, and 4.6 percent.

The impact of coal mining on downstream utility MCL violations for nitrates, arsenic, and general IOC contaminants is presented in Table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_minevio_mcl_ivsum_binvio}. The IV regression of the effect of the number of coal mines upstream estimates statistically and economically insignificant changes in the likelihood a utility experiences an arsenic or nitrate MCL violation. The point estimate for the effect of an additional mine on the likelihood a utility spends a year in a nitrate MCL violation is $-0.02$ percentage points, and for an inorganic chemical MCL it is $-0.7$ percentage points. None of these effects are statistically significant due to the extreme rarity of MCLs in the sample.

Upstream coal mining increases MR violations without increasing MCL violations. Despite all the significant effects of coal mining being concentrated in MR violations and not in MCL violations, I cannot conclude that coal mining does not impact actual contaminant levels. A maximum contaminant level violation is only issued by the regulator when they receive the results of a test that a utility conducted. Consistently failing to conduct tests or report the results of tests and incurring an MR violation prevents an MCL from being issued, even if contaminant concentration legitimately exceeds the MCL. The SDWA ECHO violation dataset is, for that reason, unable to measure drinking water contamination when systems do not monitor and report tests.

The theoretical model predicts that lapses in self reporting can occur without contaminant concentration violations occurring when contamination increases following mining and testing costs rise or utilities seek to avoid MCL penalties by hiding MCL violations in MR violations, or if penalties fall. I next test whether contamination rises with mining.

\section{The impact of coal mining on downstream utility contamination}

\subsection{Empirical Framework}

I estimate the effect of coal mining on IOC water contaminant concentrations at utilities. This tests whether utilities violated the SDWA due to rising compliance costs. Upstream mining increases the costs of compliance by raising the level of pollution and the effort that needs to be expended to stay in compliance. As the costs of compliance rise, the cost savings and, therefore, the benefits of non-compliance rise too. I limit the estimation to arsenic, nitrates, barium, chromium, and selenium. The MCL for these IOC contaminants did not change between 1985 and 2005 and were the IOC contaminants that were kept after cleaning the SYR2 data.

I estimate the impact of coal mining on the mean concentration of contaminants within downstream utilities using the equation

\begin{equation}\label{eq:huc02reg}
    \textit{Concen}_{cht}= \beta\cdot\sum_{\tau=1985}^t\text{upstream coal prod}_{c\tau}  + \rho_c + HUC02_h\cdot\tau_t + \epsilon_{cht}
\end{equation}

Where $\textit{Concen}_{cht}$ is the mean concentration of a contaminant measured at utility c in HUC02 h in year t using SYR2 data from 1997 to 2005. $\sum_{\tau=1985}^t\text{upstream coal prod}_{c\tau}$ is the cumulative tons of coal mined in all HUC12s directly upstream of all utility c intakes from 1985 until year t. $\beta$ is the effect of an additional 10,000 tons of coal mined upstream of utility c. Utility fixed effects are included as $\rho_c$, and to control for annual average pollution levels within the HUC02 watershed, I include HUC02 interacted with time fixed effects $HUC02_h\cdot\tau_t$. Standard errors are clustered at the utility level.

Equation \ref{eq:huc02reg} is a continuous differences-in-differences regression with no pure control observations. The parallel trends assumption requires that the pollution concentrations at utilities that experience a large decline in coal production would have trended the same as the pollution concentrations at utilities with a small decline.

\subsection{Results}

\input{reg/6yr_huc02fe_inorg_ravalli_2005}

Table \ref{tab:6yr_huc02fe_inorg_ravalli_2005} contains the results of regression \ref{eq:huc02reg} on IOC contaminant concentration tests throughout utility water processing stages. An additional 10 million cumulative short tons of coal mined upstream since 1985 increases mean concentrations of IOC contaminants at utilities. The effect is significant for all IOC contaminants except for nitrates, which I lack the power to identify at a statistically significant level.

Arsenic increases by 0.0023 mg/L after an additional 10 million short tons of coal mined upstream. This is economically significant, equivalent to almost a 100 percent increase over the mean arsenic concentration of 0.0029 mg/L, and is statistically significant. The effect represents 23 percent of the EPA arsenic MCL of 0.010 mg/L after 2005 and 4.6 percent of the MCL before 2006, which is 0.050 mg/L.

An additional 10 million cumulative short tons of coal increases chromium, selenium, and barium by 0.0027 mg/L, 0.0033 mg/L, and 0.0171 mg/L, respectively. The effects are statistically significant and large relative to the mean contaminant levels, representing increases of 50 percent, 69 percent, and 24 percent, respectively.

I lack the power to identify the effect of an additional 10 million cumulative short tons of coal mined upstream on nitrates. An additional 10 million short tons of coal increases nitrate concentrations by 0.05 mg/L; however, the effect is not statistically significant. The effect represents a 5 percent increase in concentration levels relative to the mean contaminant concentration of 0.75 mg/L. Assuming the contaminant concentrations increase linearly with each additional 10 million short tons of coal mined, an increase in coal production upstream of utility from zero to 100 million short tons would result in the nitrate contaminant concentration exceeding 50 percent of the nitrate MCL. The maximum short tons of coal produced upstream of a utility during this time period is 10 million short tons. 

Mining increases mean IOC contaminant concentrations (by 20--100 percent
of mean concentrations). Mining raises utility costs to completely remove contaminants. Utilities downstream of mines face costlier SDWA compliance. Despite the economic and statistically significant result that upstream coal mining raises IOC contaminant concentrations at utilities, there are no observations in the SYR2 data that exceed the MCL of any IOCs; see Table \ref{tab:6yr_huc02fe_inorg_val_sumstats_ravalli_2005}. This does not confirm that water contamination never exceeded the MCL. Most MR violations were issued for failure to test water quality. The SYR2 necessarily reflects the results from water tests and does not account for tests not undertaken that were subsequently punished with an MR violation.

I estimate the effect of SYR2 contaminant concentrations of nitrates on the likelihood of nitrate self reporting violations. After 1991, the cost of self reporting rose with contaminant concentrations. Nitrate self reporting requirements required increased monitoring if nitrate concentrations were detected at 50 percent of the nitrate MCL.

Table \ref{tab:mr_concentration_lag} reports regressions of linear probability models of the likelihood of a utility failing to monitor and report their water for nitrate levels within 6 months and a year of a contaminant reading. I find that when nitrate contaminant readings are greater than 50 percent of the nitrate MCL, a utility is 0.5 percentage points more likely to fail to self report water tests for nitrates in the following year and 0.2 percentage points more likely to fail to monitor and report nitrate water tests within six months. The failures after a nitrate reading has exceeded 50 percent occur during a time of enhanced nitrate monitoring at the utility since these results are from 1997 to 2005. This suggests that utilities are more likely to fail to keep up with marginal increases in monitoring and reporting schedules. Enforcement does not increase.

To investigate the penalties and disincentives for missing self reporting requirements, I estimate the impact of coal mining on the likelihood that a utility receives enforcement and regulatory visits. The coefficient on coal mines is not interpreted as regulators directly responding to coal mining upstream, but rather as the violations that occur at the utility as a result of coal mining.

Formal enforcement is a large cost burden to utilities. It involves fines, court orders, and prosecution. To summarize the likelihood of receiving formal enforcement Table \ref{tab:sanitary_visit_timing_formal_enforcement_rate} calculates the rate of formal enforcement at utilities downstream of coal mines. Rates are calculated across the utility-year panel. Formal enforcement is more likely to be used when a utility fails to self report, and formal enforcement is even more likely to be used when a utility fails to self-report and receives a sanitary visit. \subsection{Instrumental variable}

I have established that coal mining increases utilities' violations of the SDWA. Violations increase as contaminant concentrations rise, which I have also established occurs when mining increases. The theoretical model predicts that violations will rise when enforcement or penalties for violations fall. I estimate whether regulatory enforcement action is less costly as coal mining increases upstream of utilities. 

Coal mine siting and production could be endogenous to regulator enforcement of SDWA at utilities. For example, utilities that are able to protect their water sources from upstream mining would, on average, be better able to reduce the cost of regulatory enforcement they would face from SDWA violations. To address the endogeneity of mine siting and regulator action at utilities, the number of upstream coal mines is instrumented with $\text{UpstreamCoalSulfur\%}_c \cdot \text{Post95}_t$ as in Equation \ref{1ststage2sls}. I estimate the effect of upstream coal mining on the likelihood of formal and informal enforcement using IV for upstream coal mining. Formal enforcement is more severe and costlier than informal enforcement. 

Part of any effect that I find of coal mining on regulator enforcement of the SDWA at utilities is the effect of water pollution from coal mining on regulator enforcement. Regulators are more likely responding to pollution caused by upstream coal mining rather than by upstream coal mining itself since utility water quality is regulated by state and federal authorities, and mine safety is regulated by the federal MSHA. It is possible that state environmental regulators are aware of coal mining pollution through their primacy to enforce point source pollution in the Clean Water Act. Coal mines are point source polluters. Regulators may coordinate their response to the utilities affected by coal mining with their knowledge of coal production itself.

The first stage is
\begin{equation}\label{1ststage2slsreg}
\begin{split}
\text{NumMines}_{ct} &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1\text{NumIntakes}_{ct} + \alpha_2\text{UpstreamCoalSulfur\%}_c \cdot \text{Post95}_t \\
&\quad + Y_t + C_c + \nu_{ct}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

And the second stage is
\begin{equation}\label{2ndstage2slsreg}
\begin{split}
\text{Regulatory Action}_{ct} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1\text{NumIntakes}_{ct} + \gamma\widehat{\text{NumMines}}_{ct} \\
&\quad + Y_t + C_c + \epsilon_{ct}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The Number of Mines that are one HUC12 upstream of a utility c water intake in year t, $NumMines_{ct}$, are predicted by a function of the number of utility intake facilities, $NumIntakes_{ct}$, the average coal sulfur percentage in upstream HUC12s where coal sulfur can be calculated, $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c$, interacted with a dummy indicator for the years after 1995, stage I of the ARP, $Post95_t$, year fixed effects, $Y_t$, utility fixed effects, $C_c$, and an error term, $\nu_{ct}$. Standard errors are clustered at the utility level.

The utility fixed effects control for the effect that upstream sulfur is associated with a higher baseline of AMD which could increase pollution and therefore enforcement action outside of the effect of the instrument on coal mines.

The exclusion restriction fails when the instrument affects regulator behavior through another path outside of its effect on coal mining. The exclusion restriction would be threatened if the ARP Phase I directly changed state EPA enforcement practices in a way that affected utilities differently according to the percent of sulfur by weight in upstream coal. However, states were not primarily responsible for enforcing the ARP; it was a federally regulated program that, by itself, would not have affected state primacy enforcement of the SDWA.

Any national regulatory changes to the SDWA that affected all utilities are controlled for with time fixed effects. The threat to the exogeneity of the instrument would occur if regulatory enforcement in low sulfur regions before and after 1995 changed differently from the change in regulatory enforcement in high sulfur regions before and after 1995.

Table \ref{tab:h3_inf_formal_d12} presents the coefficients from estimating Equation \ref{2ndstage2slsreg} on the likelihood of formal and informal enforcement. Upstream coal mining leads to a reduction in formal enforcement and an effect close to zero for informal enforcement; however, I lack the power to identify this effect with any statistical significance.

The OLS regression measures a 1.69 percentage point fall in any formal enforcement occurring at a utility in the year when an additional mine operates upstream. The IV estimates an additional upstream coal mine leads to a 5.6 percentage point fall in the likelihood of a utility receiving formal enforcement action. The omitted variable bias is positive and is consistent with the bias from omitting utility ability to protect upstream water sources and influence SDWA regulatory enforcement. 

The coefficient of $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \times Post95_t$ in the reduced form regression compares the enforcement at utilities with higher sulfur levels after 1995 to those with lower sulfur levels. A one-percentage point increase in $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c$ is associated with a 1.45 percentage point increase in formal enforcement after 1995, compared to before 1995. Mining declines with sulfur after 1995; this means that places that likely experienced declines in mining after 1995 also experienced increases in formal enforcement compared to places that experienced mining increases. Formal enforcement is less likely to be used as upstream mining increases. 

The regulator necessarily lacks information about the quality of water at a utility incurring an MR violation. If the actual contaminant concentration is above the MCL then the violation requires urgent action to protect public health. However, if the actual contaminant concentration is below the MCL, then the violation does not require urgent action to protect public health. The regulator can use sanitary inspections of utilities to determine the risk to public health and determine the type of action that must be taken to enforce compliance.

Table \ref{tab:h2_snsv_d12} presents the coefficients from estimating Equation \ref{2ndstage2slsreg} on the likelihood of a regulator conducting a sanitary visit to a utility following an increase in upstream coal mining. Coal mining leads to a 14 percentage point increase in sanitary visits by the regulator to the utility. This is statistically significant at the 1 percent level. I cannot observe the findings of these sanitary visits due to missing data.

\section{Discussion}

Coal mining increases the contaminant concentration of inorganic chemicals at drinking water utilities. Self reporting costs rise due to more required tests for the same contaminants and for more contaminants required to self report. Utilities self report at lower rates as self reporting costs rise. 

However, this result is limited to a subset of utilities and a subset of the time period.

Coal mining contamination does not raise contaminant concentrations above MCLs in the limited SYR2 data. This suggests that utilities are not reducing rates of self reporting due to hiding MCLs. The sanitary visits that occur as coal mining increases and self reporting falls may reveal to the regulator that the risk to public health from non self reporting utilities is low. Utilities do not receive more penalties after receiving regulators' sanitary visits and increasing their rate of non self reporting. This suggests that the penalties for utilities not self reporting are too low to deter the reduction in self reporting that occurs from self reporting cost increases.

Utilities are off-loading the responsibility of self reporting onto regulators. The welfare effects of this are not a priori clear. The regulators may be able to monitor with economies of scale that are lower than the utilities cost of self reporting. However, the economies of scale would have to overcome the large amount of resources the regulator would have to devote to monitoring all utilities in a timely manner.
\section{Conclusion}

Under a self reporting regulatory framework designed to monitor a quality target, self reporting falls if the quality exogenously deteriorates even if that quality does not fall below regulatory standards. I have shown this in the context of coal mining and utilities: utility SDWA self reporting of water quality falls as exogenous water quality deteriorates. Utility SDWA self reporting becomes costlier as coal mining increases, since an increase in coal mining leads to increases in inorganic chemical water contamination at utilities, which increases the cost of self monitoring. I do not detect mean contaminant levels increasing above maximum contaminant levels and cannot conclude that upstream coal mining is increasing pollution to the extent that utilities need to install improved abatement technology to meet SDWA maximum contaminant level compliance. 

I find that penalties for not self reporting are not high enough to reduce the incentive for utilities to reduce self reporting as self reporting costs rise. The regulator can use sanitary visits to assess the threat posed to public health by an MR violation and can implement a less costly type of enforcement when the threat is discovered to be low. However, without punishing non self reporting at high levels, regulators will have to conduct more sanitary visits to ensure that contaminant concentration levels do not exceed health limits.
