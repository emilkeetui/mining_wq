\title{The Impact of Coal Mining on Drinking Water Quality, Monitoring, and Violations}
\author[1]{Emil Kee-Tui and Nicolas Ramkumar}

\begin{center}\begin{abstract}
		\noindent A large share of global energy is still supplied by coal. Coal mining will persist to meet that demand, which in the US is supported by federal policy intervention. We study the effect of coal mining on drinking water quality at Community Water Systems (CWS), an important vector for the health impacts of any water pollution, since a vast majority of US households obtain their water from a CWS. We use an instrumental variable to address concerns that the siting of coal mining is endogenous to the water quality and performance of CWSs. The instrument uses the national shift in demand for coal with a lower sulfur content that occurred after Stage I of the 1990 Clean Air Act amendments, better known as the acid rain program. We find that an additional coal mine upstream of a CWS causes a large and significant increase in CWS violations of the Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA). We find that mining increases violations by raising the costs of compliance with the SDWA through increasing mean concentrations of inorganic chemical contaminants. Regulators do not impose costly enforcement that might discourage the increasing violations and are more likely to use less costly enforcement when pollution increases from upstream coal mining. These results suggest that coal mining raises CWS violations because compliance costs increase and penalties for non-compliance decrease with upstream coal mining.

\end{abstract}\end{center}

\section{Introduction}

The environmental and health costs of coal will continue into the near future as US and global coal consumption and policy support coal mining and coal fired electricity generation. Coal was used to generate 16 percent of US electricity and one third of global electricity in 2024 (\cite{IEA2025GER}). US federal energy policy under president Donald Trump has expanded mining access on federal lands and intervened to prevent planned coal fired power plant's retirements (\cite{Trump2025EO14260}; \cite{Trump2025EO14261};\cite{Trump2025Proc10914}; \cite{Daly2025CoalMining}). Pollution from coal mining will primarily affect the communities that live in close proximity to the mines, unlike coal fired electricity generation, which produces global greenhouse gasses along with local air quality pollution. This paper asks whether US coal mining degrades the drinking water quality of downstream communities and whether drinking water regulation protects those communities.

For the 85\% of US households that receive their water from utilities known as community water systems (CWS) the impact of coal mining on human health is determined by the response of CWSs to water contamination. CWSs are regulated by the Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) of 1974 to remove contaminants for certain chemicals and pollutants below levels that pose a risk to human health. CWSs are required to remove several inorganic chemical contaminants that can be produced during coal mining through acid mine drainage. Acid mine drainage (AMD) occurs when sulfide-bearing rocks are exposed during mining, oxidize on contact with air and water, produce sulfuric acid, dissolve heavy metals, and leach into surface and groundwater (\cite{skousen2019acid}). Rising IOC pollution increases the costs CWSs must spend to remove the contamination and remain compliant with the SDWA. Both privately owned and publicly owned CWSs are incentivized to lower costs; private CWSs have a profit maximizing incentive, and public CWSs are incentivized to avoid raising utility rates to pay rising costs. How CWSs respond when pollution removal costs increase due to rising pollution determines the health consequences of coal mining on drinking water.

CWSs are required to self-monitor their water quality for regulated contaminants and self-report their monitoring results to the regulator. Regulators issue a maximum contaminant level (MCL) violation to a CWS when the reported concentration of a regulated contaminant exceeds levels safe for human health. MCL violations often trigger costly enforcement responses, such as compliance agreements, consent decrees, and treatment infrastructure upgrades. Regulators cannot issue MCL violations unless CWSs self-monitor and self-report their water quality. Monitoring and reporting is a costly requirement in and of itself, requiring the hiring of technical staff to collect samples, laboratories to process samples, and administrative staff to submit test results and follow the testing schedule deadlines. To encourage CWSs to self-monitor and self-report water quality tests, regulators issue monitoring and reporting (MR) violations when a CWS fails to test or submit tests to the regulator. MR violations are penalized far less harshly, such as with a notice of violation or a public notification requirement. A CWS facing rising compliance costs due to upstream pollution has a strategic option: by failing to test or report, it incurs a less costly MR violation while avoiding the detection that would trigger a more costly MCL enforcement response. 

Rising upstream pollution raises the costs of removing contaminants, making it costlier to avoid an MCL violation and increasing the need to test and report water, thereby raising costs spent on monitoring and reporting. The benefits to a CWS from not testing and reporting, or from delaying a test or a report, are the cost savings on lab tests, personnel, administrative costs, and avoiding an MCL violation if the tests would reveal a concentration above an MCL. The benefits to a CWS from avoiding or delaying an MCL are the cost savings from avoiding costly enforcement, which may require financing and installing new treatment infrastructure or raising rates to pay for their installation. Whether rising compliance costs due to upstream pollution lead CWSs to change their compliance with the SDWA is an open empirical question. Utilizing MR violations over MCLs obscures pollution, precisely in the cases where they are most severe.

Identifying the causal effect of upstream mining on CWS behavior is complicated by two sources of endogeneity. First, coal mines are located in areas with particular geological characteristics, such as coal seam accessibility and transportation corridors, that may also independently predict water quality and CWS characteristics. This may understate the true effect of coal mining on CWS violations and water quality. Second, CWSs with more political influence may successfully resist upstream mine siting, and those same systems may be better at maintaining compliance, generating a negative correlation between upstream mining and CWS ability that understates the true compliance burden mining imposes. This leads to OLS underestimating the effect of coal mines on CWS violations.

We address these identification challenges by instrumenting for the location and production of coal mines using the Acid Rain Program (ARP), enacted as part of the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990. The ARP required large coal-fired power plants, the dominant consumers of coal, accounting for approximately 90 percent of US coal demand, to reduce their sulfur dioxide emissions, with compliance for Phase I of the ARP mandatory by 1995. The least costly way for most plants to comply was to switch from high-sulfur coal to low-sulfur coal with less than two percent sulfur content by weight. This created a sharp, nationally-determined shift in coal demand: after 1995, demand for high-sulfur coal fell substantially, while demand for low-sulfur coal increased. As a result, coal production declined in high-sulfur mining areas and expanded in low-sulfur areas. Our instrument interacts a post-1995 indicator with the average sulfur content of coal deposits in the watershed directly upstream of each CWS's water intake. This interaction predicts changes in upstream mine activity driven by the ARP's differential demand shock across areas with different coal sulfur content and is orthogonal to the unobserved characteristics of CWSs that determine compliance behavior. The ARP was a national air quality regulation drafted independently of any consideration of drinking water quality; its timing and design were determined to address acid rain and electricity generator compliance timelines and were unrelated to local CWS characteristics, SDWA regulatory changes, or the ability of any CWS to manage local water contamination.

We construct a panel from 1985 to 2005 linking CWSs to annual coal production occurring in the watershed upstream of their water intakes. Mine locations come from the Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA); annual mine-level production from the Energy Information Administration (EIA); watershed coal sulfur content from USGS COALQUAL; watershed connectivity from USGS NHDplus; and CWS violation and enforcement records from the EPA's Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS). We supplement these data with measured contaminant concentrations from the EPA's second Six-Year Review (SYR2), which collected compliance monitoring readings at CWSs from 1998 to 2005. This concentration dataset allows us to directly verify that upstream coal production raises inorganic chemical contamination at CWSs independent of violation behavior.

We find that coal mining raises CWS inorganic chemical (IOC) monitoring and reporting (MR) violations, failures to conduct and report required water quality tests. An additional active coal mine one watershed upstream of a CWS's water intake increases the number of days per year a CWS spends in violation of the SDWA by approximately 14 to 20 days, depending on the IOC contaminant. The 2SLS coefficients on MCL violations are statistically and economically insignificant for all contaminants, with point estimates an order of magnitude below those for MR violations. Coal mining significantly raises the incidence of non-monitoring but has no effect on increasing detected contaminant exceedances above contaminant limits.

The increase in MR violations is driven by an increase in contaminant concentration, which increases the benefit of non-compliance. Higher levels of pollution raise the benefit of non-compliance: MCL is more likely at higher levels of pollution, so avoiding testing avoids potentially high penalties. Treatment costs higher as pollution levels rise, increasing the cost savings from every level of negligence. Upstream coal mining measurably raises actual IOC concentrations in CWS water. Every additional 10 million short tons of coal mined upstream since 1985 increases mean arsenic concentrations by 0.0023 mg/L, approximately 100 percent of the average arsenic concentration level in the sample, as well as chromium by 50 percent, selenium by 69 percent, and barium by 24 percent of their respective sample means. These effects are economically and statistically significant. The concentrations measured in the SYR2 data are drawn from water tests that were conducted and reported, but the majority of SDWA violations in our sample are issued precisely for failing to conduct such tests. The true contamination distribution during MR violation periods is unobserved by regulators and researchers alike, and the rising concentration evidence suggests the worst contamination is concentrated during the periods of non-monitoring.

The increase in MR violations downstream of coal mining is also driven by a reduction in enforcement penalties. An additional upstream mine reduces the probability that a CWS receives formal enforcement action, the more costly regulatory response relative to informal enforcement action, by 5.6 percentage points, while increasing the probability of a sanitary visit by 14 percentage points. Regulators substitute less punitive responses as upstream pollution rises. This is consistent with a regulatory system in which the social cost of formal enforcement, including the potential system disruptions and capital expenditures it imposes, leads regulators to exercise discretion toward lenient responses when the compliance burden on CWSs is high, but it reinforces rather than offsets the non-compliance behavior of CWSs facing rising contamination costs.

The rising benefits of non-compliance and the falling penalties of the non-compliance act reinforce each other to increase SDWA non-compliance with upstream coal mining. Adapting the regulatory compliance framework of \cite{kang2021understanding}, we show that CWSs choose higher negligence levels, conduct fewer tests, and submit fewer reports to regulators when compliance costs rise and when penalties fall. Our empirical results confirm both: mining raises contamination and thus increases the cost of maintaining compliance; and it also triggers softer enforcement responses that reduce the expected penalty from non-compliance.

These results contribute to three strands of the literature. The first concerns the environmental consequences of coal mining and natural resource extraction. Existing work documents coal mining's effects on air quality, greenhouse gas emissions, and health (\cite{muller2011environmental}; \cite{parfitt2024there}), and finds evidence of water quality degradation from mining in international settings (\cite{aragon2016polluting}; \cite{chu2023air}). Our paper provides the first causal estimates of coal mining's downstream effect on the drinking water quality of treated water at CWSs in the United States, identifying that coal mining pollution crosses the gap from environmental contamination to piped household water.

The second contribution is to the literature on strategic environmental self-monitoring and reporting. Firms regulated by environmental standards face incentives to under-report emissions when the penalties for revelation exceed the penalties for non-disclosure (\cite{duflo2013truth}; \cite{duflo2018value}; \cite{mu2024s}; \cite{helland1998enforcement}). CWSs are a distinctive setting: unlike the firms in this literature, they are not the source of the pollution they are regulated to remove. Their incentive to undermonitor arises not from hiding their own emissions but from avoiding the detection of upstream contamination that would require costly remediation. This generates a clean identification opportunity: the ARP-driven change in upstream coal mining is plainly exogenous to the CWS's own historical reporting behavior, ruling out the reverse causality concerns that complicate identification in contexts where the firm controls both the pollution and its measurement. We provide direct evidence that MR violations rise alongside measured contamination increases, strengthening the causal inference that non-monitoring is strategic rather than accidental. Prior work on CWS strategic violations has relied on violations data alone (\cite{wallsten2008effects}; \cite{foster2019arsenic}). The joint observation of rising contaminant concentrations, rising MR violations, and falling formal enforcement, all causally attributed to the same upstream production shock, provides substantially stronger evidence of strategic compliance behavior than prior studies.

The third contribution is to the literature on US drinking water quality and the SDWA. Water quality in the US has improved substantially since the passage of the Clean Water Act and SDWA (\cite{keiser2019consequences}); yet, between 9 and 34 million people per year are served by CWSs in violation of health-based standards (\cite{allaire2018national}). Our findings suggest that the regulatory design of the SDWA, which relies on CWS self-monitoring and assigns substantially lower penalties to non-monitoring than to detected contamination, creates systematic incentives to undertest in precisely the circumstances where testing matters most. Strengthening the monitoring schedule or increasing penalties for non-monitoring in high-pollution areas may be a more effective lever for improving drinking water outcomes than increases in MCL stringency alone.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides background on the Acid Rain Program, coal mining water pollutants, the SDWA, and regulator enforcement behavior. Section 3 presents the theoretical framework. Section 4 describes the data. Section 5 documents the shift in CWS exposure to coal production induced by the ARP. Section 6 presents our main results on the effect of coal mining on SDWA violations. Section 7 estimates the effect of upstream coal mining on measured contaminant concentrations. Section 8 examines regulator enforcement responses to coal-affected CWSs. Section 9 concludes.

\section{Background}

\subsection{The Acid Rain Program (ARP)}

We use an instrumental variable to address the endogeneity of the location of coal mines. The instrumental variable uses the change in demand preferences for coal based on sulfur content that followed the sulfur dioxide restrictions imposed on coal fired power plants from the Clean Air Act Amendments, better known as the Acid Rain Program (ARP). The ARP was signed into law on November 15, 1990, to reduce sulfur dioxide (SO2) and nitrogen oxides (NOx) emissions, the primary precursors of acid rain, from fossil fuel burning power plants. The regulation set total annual US SO2 and NOx emissions goals at 10 million and 2 million fewer tons, respectively, below their 1980 emission levels. The emissions reductions were achieved by creating an emissions cap and-trade market, installing scrubbers on power plants, and switching fuel sources for power generators. The ARP achieved 100\% compliance and had the intended effect of reducing acid rain (\cite{lattanzio2022clean}). 

Emissions were capped in two phases. In phase I, the EPA issued emission permits and allowances to 110 high polluting, mostly coal-fired electricity generating facilities. Facilities targeted under phase I were required to achieve compliance with SO2 emission reductions by 1995. Facilities were required to install SO2 and NOx monitors and were fined when their emissions exceeded their allotted emission limits or the sum of emission limits they purchased on the SO2 and NOx trading markets from other emitters. Phase II included smaller power generating units that used oil, coal, and gas. The deadline for compliance with phase II was January 1, 2000. We focus on the facilities affected by phase I because they are the largest power plants and coal consumers.

As opposed to SO2 emissions, the ARP did not limit total NOx emissions from sources; instead, it limited the NOx emissions per mmBTU of fossil fuels burned. NOx limits affected coal fired facilities at the same time they came under SO2 emission limits. Phase I NOx rules were promulgated in 1995. Phase II NOx regulation was promulgated in 2000.

Fuel switching to low sulfur coal was a primary method by which electricity generators met the ARP requirements. Since the electric power industry accounted for 90\% of coal demand in 2000 (\cite{freme2000us}), the ARP increased the demand for low sulfur coal and reduced the demand for high sulfur coal (\cite{epaarpcoalemp}; \cite{stratford2015coalprod}). 

\subsection{Coal mining water pollutants}

Environmental pollution from coal mining can include heavy metals, radioactive elements, non-organic compound pollutants, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in the environment (\cite{rouhani2023overview};\cite{boente2020benzo}; \cite{liang2017spatial}). Inorganic chemical pollution is commonly produced during coal mining through acid mine drainage. Acid mine drainage occurs when rocks containing sulfides are exposed to air and water, beginning the process of oxidation and decomposition (\cite{ceto2000abandoned}). The oxidized minerals form sulfuric acid which dissolves metal ions and sulphates that can then be carried into surface or groundwater (\cite{akcil2006acid}; \cite{skousen2019acid}). Acid mine drainage is more common when the minerals contain high levels of sulfur, making the incidence of acid mine drainage increase with coal sulfur levels (\cite{schweinfurth2009introduction}). Mines can reduce the incidence of AMD by covering exposed tailings and excavation from the air with water, low-sulfide soils, clay, cement, and organic matter. AMD can be treated once it has taken place, and treatment options include actively adding alkaline chemicals, constructing wetlands, and installing filters \cite{KEFENI2017475}.

\subsection{Community Water Systems (CWS), regulators, and the Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA)}

Community water systems (CWS) are utilities that provide water to at least 25 consumers through at least 15 connections throughout the year. They can be privately or publicly owned, source their water from groundwater, surface water, or another CWS, and serve small rural communities as well as the largest cities. The Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) is the federal regulation governing water quality at all CWS; it establishes the regulatory floor and can be complemented by state CWS regulations. State agencies are responsible for enforcing CWS compliance with testing, reporting, and water quality standards. The USEPA oversees state enforcement of the SDWA and promulgates lists of contaminants that CWSs must monitor and remove from their water. 

The SDWA limits the concentrations of contaminants within CWS water by setting maximum contaminant levels (MCL). We focus on inorganic chemical (IOC) pollutants as a general category of pollutants and several individual IOC pollutants with MCL's that did not change between 1985 and 2005. IOC pollutants are directly associated with coal mining through acid mine drainage. Between 1985 and 2005, the EPA promulgated 20 rule changes that expanded the list of regulated contaminants. The SDWA MCL requirements for arsenic, nitrates, barium, chromium, and selenium remained the same between 1985 and 2005. The other inorganic chemicals regulated between 1985 and 2005 were cadmium, lead, mercury, silver, antimony, asbestos, Beryllium, cyanide, nickel, and nitrite, some of which were added to the list of regulated contaminants by 2005. 

A CWS receives an MCL violation and enforcement action by the state regulator when it reports an exceeded MCL. States are responsible for bringing violating CWSs into SDWA compliance. Except for a subset of violating circumstances and mandatory notifications to consumers that CWS must provide when in violation, the SDWA gives states wide discretionary power to tailor compliance and enforcement plans to each CWS and violation, with the intention that the state regulator would bring the CWS into compliance with the MCL quickly to reduce health impacts of pollution. Enforcement frequently escalates from informal compliance assistance (letters, notices of violation, compliance plans) and, if violations persist, leads to formal administrative actions. Enforcement is frequently remedial rather than punitive, and compliance plans directly addressing the cause of the violation are more common than civil or criminal financial penalties. Compliance plans that require installing treatment infrastructure or hiring technical personnel are still costly to the CWS. 

The SDWA relies on CWSs to self-monitor and report their water quality to regulators on schedule for each contaminant. In 1991, the MR testing schedule underwent a significant change by establishing testing compliance cycles for contaminants. In 1991, CWSs were required to conduct tests for four consecutive quarters to establish baseline levels for contaminants if they could not show there was no risk of contamination and had not tested since 1990. After initial testing, groundwater systems were required to test every three years, and surface water systems were required to test annually (56 FR 3526, 1991). The first three-year compliance period ran from 1993 to 1995, the second ran from 1996 to 1998, and the third compliance period ran from 1999 to 2001. The next nine year timeline began in 2002, with the first cycle ending in 2004 and the next cycle starting in 2005.

A CWS receives a monitoring and reporting (MR) violation when it fails to test, monitor, and submit water test results to regulators on schedule. A CWS that does not monitor is not presumed to be in violation of an MCL, only of the monitoring requirement itself. MR violation enforcement action is generally less costly to the CWS than MCL violations. MR violations receive less costly enforcement than MCL violations of the same contaminant, in some cases MR violations receive no enforcement action at all. MR violation enforcement starts with low cost actions such as notices of violation, can include more costly informal actions such as requiring public notifications, and can escalate to costly formal actions such as consent decrees and penalties.

CWSs found monitoring and reporting requirements to be difficult to meet due to the administrative and technical burden. The CWS needed to be administratively organized to conduct the right tests and report them on schedule. Technical staff were required to conduct separate tests for contaminants or to prepare samples to be sent to laboratories. A survey of Indiana CWS managers who responded to a 1980 survey on the impact of the SDWA said that the SDWA's reporting requirements had the greatest impact on their systems, while MCL had the least (\cite{oleckno1982national}). 

Except for a subset of violating circumstances and mandatory notifications to consumers that CWSs must provide when in violation, the SDWA gives primacy states wide discretionary power to tailor compliance plans to each CWS and violation. States can design and implement their own enforcement procedures. In theory, each state could design a tailored compliance plan for a particular violation at each particular CWS. Enforcement usually took the form of compliance plans requiring the CWS to directly address the cause of the violation and rarely included civil or criminal penalties. A tailored enforcement plan would improve the return to compliance since each pollutant may require a different technology, and there may exist multiple technologies that have different costs or technical requirements that a CWS may or may not be able to maintain or pay for, depending on the resources and staff at the CWS. Compliance plans could still be costly to the CWS if they required installing treatment infrastructure or conducting tests. 

The few enforcement tools directly specified in the SDWA were civil actions through citizen suits and CWS public notifications of violations. Civil actions in courts could force any government agency or CWS to undertake action to comply with the SDWA or face civil penalties. Between 1974 and 1995, fines were capped at \$5,000 per violation per day; the 1996 Amendments raised this to \$25,000 per day (adjusted for inflation in subsequent years). Civil or criminal judicial enforcement was rare (\cite{shimshack2014economics}). Ordinary SDWA noncompliance was not subject to criminal liability; criminal provisions are reserved for tampering with a water system (up to 20 years imprisonment, 42 USC §300i-1) and willful underground injection control violations.

The SDWA also enforced compliance by requiring CWS to notify consumers of any violations, variances, or exemptions they had received. variances and exemptions allowed a CWS to not comply with an MCL or MR requirement if the state determined that there were limited health effects from allowing the variance and that the risk of the contaminant was low in that CWS's source water. After the 1996 SDWA amendments, all CWS had to send consumer confidence reports to their customers. These reputational compliance tools have been shown to be effective at inducing compliance at CWSs (\cite{bennear2008impacts}).

\section{Theoretical Framework}

We model the effect of coal mining pollution on a CWS's decision to comply with the Safe Drinking Water Act under regulatory enforcement by adapting a static model of environmental compliance from \cite{kang2021understanding}, which is an adaptation of \cite{mookherjee1994marginal}. CWSs choose a negligence level $a \in [0,\bar{a}]$, which measures how much they avoid the requirements of drinking water quality regulations, from maximum negligence $\bar{a}$ to full compliance 0. Failing to conduct or submit tests to the regulator or not installing pollution abatement technology are examples of CWS negligence. Regulators do not directly observe CWSs choice of $a$. CWSs face a compliance cost, $\theta$, that is a random variable drawn from $\Theta$, which is a strictly increasing and continuously differentiable function $F(\cdot)$ with support $(0,\bar{\theta})$. CWSs know their draw of $\theta$, but regulators only know the distribution of $\Theta$. A compliance cost is determined by both a CWS's ability to manage a drinking water system, such as the skills of managerial and technical staff, and by the costs of water treatment, including the cost of filtration equipment or sterilization chemicals.

CWSs receive a benefit $\theta b(a)$ from having compliance costs $\theta$ and choosing a negligence level $a$. A CWS's benefits from not conducting a test for a specific contaminant are the wages and lab fees for sampling that it saved. The opportunity cost to the CWS of choosing a compliance level $a$ relative to maximum negligence $\bar{a}$ is $\theta [b(\bar{a})-b(a)]$. The financial benefit of a certain level of negligence to a profit maximizing privately owned CWS is obvious. Publicly owned CWSs also benefit from cost savings that allow them to forgo rate or tax increases, which may be unpopular with residents.

The number of violations a CWS incurs is a random variable $K$ that follows a Poisson distribution with mean $a$. Given the negligence level $a$, observed violations follow a Poisson process:

\[Pr(K = k | a) = e^{-a}  \frac{a^k}{k!}\]

The penalty schedule that the CWS faces for incurring $k$ violations is given by $\epsilon(k)$, which is non-decreasing and continuously differentiable. The penalty schedule is a function that includes the likelihood of the regulator enforcing the penalty at each violation and the cost of the violation. The regulator can only base the penalty on $k$ because it cannot observe negligence or the cost of compliance. Assuming the CWS is risk neutral, the expected penalty for a risk neutral CWS is 

\begin{equation}
    e(a)\equiv exp(-a)\sum^\infty_{k=0}\frac{\epsilon(k)}{k!}a^k.
\end{equation}

The expected payoff to the CWS from negligence level $a$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cws_benefitcost}
    -\theta(m)[b(\bar{a})-b(a)]-e(a).
\end{equation}

A negligence schedule, $a(\cdot)$, is implemented by a penalty schedule, $e(\cdot)$, if $a(\theta)$ maximizes equation \ref{eq:cws_benefitcost} for all $\theta \in \Theta$. Combining the first order condition with the $a(\cdot)$ implemented by $e(\cdot)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{cws_foc}
    \theta b'[a(\theta)]=e'[a(\theta)]
\end{equation}
whenever $a(\theta)>0$.

Proposition 1: CWSs choose higher negligence levels as compliance costs increase, $\partial a/ \partial \theta>0$.

Assuming $b(a)$ is a strictly increasing function, $b''(a)$ is decreasing, $e''(a)$ is increasing, and the F.O.C. in equation \ref{cws_foc} solves the CWS maximization,
\[\frac{\partial a}{\partial \theta}=-\frac{b'(a)}{\theta b''(a)-e''(a)}>0\]

CWS's with higher compliance costs, for example, those with a highly polluted water source requiring high levels of treatment, find the marginal benefit of an additional unit of negligence $\theta·b'(a)$ is larger relative to the marginal expected penalty $e'(a)$. They optimally reduce compliance, and for a CWS this can be an increase in the number of monitoring and reporting violations.

Proposition 2: Suppose mining raises the concentration of pollutants at a CWS water intake, then compliance costs depend on the amount of upstream coal mining such that $\theta (m)$ is continuously differentiable and non-decreasing in m. More upstream mining raises negligence, $\partial a/\partial m >0$.
\[\frac{\partial a}{\partial m}=-\frac{\theta'(m)b'(a)}{\theta(m) b''(a)-e''(a)}>0\]

Mining raises water pollution levels, which requires increasing the costs of water treatment. As compliance costs increase, the optimal negligence level increases, testing and reporting decrease, and the number of monitoring and reporting violations increases. 

The increase in negligence and violations following an increase in coal mining can be offset by regulators increasing the enforcement of penalties for CWS violations.

Proposition 3: Higher penalties for violations increase compliance and reduce the number of monitoring and reporting violations. Assume higher penalties at every $k$ increase the penalty schedule $\epsilon(k)$ by a fixed amount $\lambda>1$, such that $\lambda\epsilon(k)$, then $\partial a/\partial \lambda<0$.

\[\frac{\partial a}{\partial \lambda}=\frac{e'(a)}{\theta b''(a)-\lambda e''(a)}<0\]

Alternatively, a reduction in the enforcement of penalties for CWS violations would increase non-compliance.

\section{Data}

\subsection{River and stream connectivity}

CWSs are exposed to water pollution from coal mining when their water intakes are downstream of coal mines. The exposure is strongest when a CWS draws from surface water. A groundwater source downstream of coal mining is also exposed to upstream pollution since groundwater is recharged by water flow from above. In order to measure which CWSs are downstream of coal mining, we measure the hydrological connectivity of mining and CWS water intakes using the USGS NHDplus watershed connectivity data. The NHDplus data identifies geographical boundaries where water that falls within that boundary would drain into the same stream segment. The NHDplus data assigns watersheds a unique 2-12 digit code known as a HUC (hydrological unit code). The number of digits corresponds to the size of the area, with the geographical area decreasing with the number of digits; for example, the lower 48 US states are divided into 18 HUC02's and over 87000 HUC12's (see figure \ref{img:WBD_Base_HUStructure_small}). HUC12 watersheds are the smallest mapped watersheds available for the entire continental US and are small enough that coal mining activities may impact the water in the HUC12 directly downstream. However, HUC12's are also large enough that activity that takes place where the HUC12 drains into another HUC12 may not affect water quality at a point upstream within the same HUC12. 

CWS water intake locations are provided at the HUC12 level by the EPA. It is possible for CWS's to obtain water from multiple HUC12's. We consider a CWS to be downstream of a coal mine if any of its water intakes are within a HUC12 directly downstream of HUC12 where mining took place between 1985 and 2005 and no water intakes are within a HUC12 where coal mining took place between 1985 and 2005.

\subsection{Coal Sulfur and Coal Mining Data}

Individual coal mine production data were obtained from the Energy Information Administration (EIA). It includes the tons of coal produced by each mine in each year from 1985 to 2005. EIA coal mines are matched to mine latitude and longitude coordinates from the Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA). Figure \ref{map:eia_msha_mine_huc12s} shows the HUC12's where mine production data from the EIA were matched to MSHA data of mine coordinates. We measure coal production in a given year as the total number of active coal mines and the total tons of coal mined within a HUC12. This aggregation can be matched to CWS water intake locations at the HUC12 level.

Our instrument predicts the location and amount of coal mining occurring upstream of a CWS using the percent of sulfur by weight in a coal bed and the time period after the compliance deadline for stage I of the ARP. We measure sulfur as a percentage of coal weight at the HUC12 level to match the upstream coal quality to CWS water intakes and the HUC12 level coal production data. We use the USGS COALQUAL data to measure the percent of sulfur by weight in a coal bed. The USGS COALQUAL dataset consists of geospatially explicit drilled core samples of coal beds from across the US, measured for characteristics including the percent of sulfur by coal weight. We match a HUC12 to all USGS COALQUAL samples within 30 km of the HUC12 boundary and average the percent of sulfur by weight for all samples matched to the HUC12. The final value of the percent of sulfur by weight in a coal bed is the average value of all samples matched to the HUC12. This approximates coal quality in watersheds that may not have coal production during the sample time period. It is reasonable to approximate that in a relatively small area, such as neighboring HUC12's, coal may be present in the ground even if it is unexploited. HUC12's are geographically small areas that, on average, have low variation in measured coal sulfur levels for samples used to estimate coal HUC12 coal sulfur levels. This reduces the mismeasurement that would occur if coal sulfur has extreme variation within HUC12s, where some high-sulfur coal HUC12s may actually host low-sulfur coal.

Figure \ref{hist:sulfurhuc12} plots a histogram of the sulfur percentage of the coal bed within the HUC12's where a CWS water intake and coal production are co-located. The sulfur content of HUC12 coal beds ranges from zero to 4.5\%, with most of the HUC12 watersheds below 1.5\%. Slightly more than half of the HUC12's are low sulfur coal beds.

HUC12's are only included in our dataset if we are able to match COALQUAL samples. Map \ref{map:map_huc12_main_sample} shows the location of HUC12's in the sample that have CWS intakes and COALQUAL samples.

\subsection{CWS characteristic, regulator, and violation data}

The EPA's Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS) Enforcement and Compliance History Online (ECHO) data contains the universe of CWS that were active continuously between 1985 and 2005. We form an annual panel of CWSs that were active between 1985 and 2005, had a water intake located in a HUC12 directly downstream from at least one active coal mine during at least one year between 1985 and 2005, and had no water intakes in a HUC12 with a coal mine that was active at any time between 1985 and 2005. This sample contains 340 unique CWSs. SDWIS ECHO contains CWS characteristics, such as the number of customers or the ownership type, as a snapshot in time when the data was last updated, often quarterly, and not as a panel. Deactivated CWSs are kept in the data, and their characteristics are not updated. This allows us to keep CWSs that were active between 1985 and 2005 but have since been deactivated in the panel. We observe the date a CWS facility, such as a water intake or treatment plant, was constructed and use this to construct a variable measuring the number of active facilities, the only time varying CWS characteristic we use.

We use the EPA's Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS) Enforcement and Compliance History Online (ECHO) data to construct outcome variables for SDWA violations, outcome variables for regulator visits, and outcome variables for regulator SDWA enforcement actions. The SDWIS violation data uniquely identifies each CWS violation of the SDWA and includes the start and end dates of the violation, the type of SDWA rule violated, the contaminant, the severity, the agency responsible for enforcing the violation, and the penalty or compliance action the agency took to bring the CWS back into compliance. We create a violation outcome variable that measures the share of the year a CWS exceeded the maximum contaminant level (MCL) or failed to meet the monitoring and reporting (MR) requirement for arsenic, nitrates, and inorganic chemical rules. We limit our analysis to SDWA contaminant violations for arsenic and nitrates due to their prevalence in the data, clear association with coal mining water contamination, and stable MCL's over the time frame. We include a general category of IOCs violations for most of the same reasons as arsenic and nitrates, although we note that between 1985 and 2005 several inorganic contaminants were added to the list of IOCs regulated under SDWA.

The share of the year spent in violation does not necessarily reflect that the CWS was violating continuously during that period. Figure \ref{img:mineviolengthhist_downstream} plots histograms of the lengths of arsenic, IOC, radionuclide, and nitrate violations. There is a tendency for the length of time of violations to align with yearly quarters. This is evidence that violations are often triggered when CWS fail to meet quarterly testing schedules and then continue for a set period of time after they are issued. Nonetheless, we measure violations using the length of time out of the year in violation to exploit variation and information that the lengths of violations contain, such as severity, with more serious violations lasting longer. We include robustness tests where a violation is coded as a binary variable equal to one if it occurred in that year and zero if it did not occur in that year.

Table \ref{tab:ioc_days_dwnstrm} presents means and standard deviations of the number of days out of a year that a CWS spends with an IOC MR and MCL violation. On average, a CWS spends between 10 to 15 days out of a year with an MR violation and less than a day with an MCL violation. This reflects that MCL violations are health dangers and are resolved as fast as possible, as well as the rarity of MCL violations, where in a given year most CWSs do not receive an MCL violation. The large standard deviation of 56 to 67 days reflects the multi-modal distribution that characterizes the distribution of the number of days a CWS is in violation. Many CWSs do not receive an MR violation in a year, and those that do tend to experience it for multiple quarters of the year.

There are only 15 MCL violations relating to arsenic, nitrate, and IOC. This makes MCL violations very rare in the data. Little can be extrapolated from 15 observations; however, most of the MCL's were issued after a single contaminant sample exceeded the threshold, and the rest were issued after the average of a group of samples exceeded the threshold.

There are 1,233 MR arsenic, nitrate, and IOC violations at the 340 CWSs between 1985 and 2005. Nitrate and general inorganic chemical violations make up 92.1\% of the 1,233 MR violations, with arsenic comprising 7.9\%. Almost all the MR violations in the data occur due to a failure to conduct regular contaminant monitoring, 99.4\%, with only a small number due to not conducting a repeat/confirmation monitoring test, 0.6\%. This is likely not a task that a resource constrained regulator can accomplish, given the number of MR violations.

\subsection{CWS Contaminant Measurement Data}

In order to measure the effect of upstream mining on actual contaminant concentrations at CWSs, we construct annual mean contaminant concentrations of several inorganic chemical contaminants in raw and treated CWS water using the second Six-Year Review data (SYR2, sample years 1998-2005). 

Starting in 1991, the USEPA began conducting reviews of all National Primary Drinking Water Regulations every six years to determine whether the rules needed revisions. The first SYR1 has not been made publicly available. The SYR2 was the second round of the six year review covering 1998 to 2005. The SYR2 data contained contaminant data for 89\% of the total population served by a drinking water system. EPA collected SYR2 data by requesting states to submit their CWS SDWA compliance data. Unrelated to the SYR and to ensure compliance with the SDWA, states were required to collect compliance monitoring data from CWSs but were not required to submit that data to the USEPA alongside the SDWA violation reports.  Pennsylvania, Mississippi, Kansas, and Louisiana were not included in the SYR2 partly due to a lack of a national database that received and stored the occurrence of contaminants at CWSs at the time.

We clean the SYR2 data following the procedure of \cite{ravalli2022sociodemographic}. We retain records from CWSs and drop transient and non-transient non-community systems. This removes roughly 23\% of raw monitoring records. For each contaminant, we compute the national share of records above the maximum laboratory detectable level (MDL), the threshold that a contaminant must be present in a sample to be detected at a laboratory, and retain only contaminants detected in more than 10\% of records. We replace the observations below the MDL of any contaminant with the MDL/$\sqrt{2}$. Records reporting a concentration greater than 100 times the contaminant's MCL are dropped as data-entry errors. Among the IOCs, this keeps arsenic, nitrate, barium, chromium, and selenium, and excludes cadmium (1.8\% detected), mercury (1.6\%), and thallium, whose concentrations are too rarely above detection. All mass-concentration readings are converted to mg/L before averaging.

After cleaning, sample-level records are collapsed to the CWS\,$\times$\,contaminant\,$\times$\,year level. The annual concentration of a particular contaminant is the mean of that year's samples at a particular CWS.

Table \ref{tab:6yr_huc02fe_inorg_val_sumstats_ravalli_2005} includes the summary statistics of the contaminants we include in our analysis. The maximum value of all contaminant readings is below their MCL. The maximum arsenic reading of 0.0110 mg/L would exceed the stricter arsenic MCL of 0.01 mg/L that became regulation in 2006 but would not exceed the pre-2006 MCL of 0.05 mg/L. That mean concentrations do not exceed MCL are consistent with the low rates of MCL violations at CWSs. Conditional on testing, the water contaminants appear to be within the regulatory limits. However, the majority of MR violations are for not conducting testing on schedule. These summary statistics do not provide information on the contaminant concentrations that would be measured when a test was required by regulation but was not conducted by the CWS.

\section{The impact of coal mining on CWS SDWA violations}

\subsection{Empirical Framework}

The SDWA forms the baseline water quality standard at CWSs. We test if CWSs stay in SDWA compliance as upstream coal mining increases. We measure the effect of upstream coal mining on CWS compliance with the SDWA using an instrumental variable regression of SDWA violations on the number of active coal mines upstream of the CWS water intake. The number of active coal mines in the HUC12 upstream of a CWS water intake is instrumented by interacting a binary variable equal to one during the time period after 1995 and zero otherwise, the compliance date for stage I of the ARP, with the percent of sulfur by weight in the coal deposits within the HUC12 directly upstream of the CWS intake. Production in low sulfur coal areas increased to meet the shifting national demand. The instrument exploits the shift in coal fired power plant's demand for coal with lower sulfur after 1995 in order to meet the sulfur dioxide emission standards of the ARP to identify variation in the location and size of coal production that is exogenous to unobserved CWS characteristics affecting violations. 

The first stage specification of our instrumental variable regression of the effect of upstream coal mining on CWS drinking water violations is

\begin{equation}\label{1ststage2sls}
\begin{split}
\text{NumMines}_{ct} &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1\text{NumIntakes}_{ct} + \alpha_2\text{UpstreamCoalSulfur\%}_c \cdot \text{Post95}_t \\
&\quad + Y_t + C_c + \nu_{ct}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Where the Number of Mines that are one HUC12 upstream of CWS c in year t is $NumMines_{ct}$. The exogenous covariates are the number of CWS intake facilities, $NumIntakes_{ct}$, year fixed effects, $Y_t$, and CWS fixed effects, $C_c$. The instrumental variable is the interaction between the average sulfur as a percentage of weight in the HUC12's directly upstream of all CWS c's water intakes, $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c$, and a dummy variable equal to 1 for the years after the deadline to implement stage I of the ARP, 1995, and 0 at other times, $Post95_t$. During the pre-1995 period, all observations of $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t$ are 0, and after 1995, the observations are the percent of sulfur in upstream coal. Standard errors are clustered at the CWS level. 

The second stage of the regression is

\begin{equation}\label{2ndstage2sls}
\begin{split}
\text{Days in Violation}_{vct} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1\text{NumIntakes}_{ct} + \gamma\widehat{\text{NumMines}}_{ct} \\
&\quad + Y_t + C_c + \epsilon_{ct}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

In the second stage, the dependent variable is the number of days in a year that CWS c experienced violation v in year t, $\text{Days in Violation}_{vct}$. 

The endogeneity of upstream sulfur is controlled for with CWS fixed effects. Sulfur is endogenous to water quality. Higher sulfur levels are associated with more sulfide minerals in the rock that oxidize and cause AMD. High sulfide rocks in the geology of a watershed naturally oxidize and leach IOCs into the water, raising the baseline contaminant level, and those rocks are more likely to contain coal seams that attract coal mining. The time invariant ability of a CWS with greater political power and resources to site their water intakes in areas with cleaner baseline ambient water quality is controlled for with CWS fixed effects.

The instrument is used to address the endogeneity in the location of coal mines with respect to water quality and the SDWA violation behavior of CWSs. The locations of CWS water intakes and coal mines are non-randomly located with respect to each other. For example, coal mines are sited where they can pass environmental reviews, and a CWS downstream of a coal mine will increase the chances of failing an environmental review. A CWS with political power may be able to influence regulators to prevent polluting industries from siting upstream, and those systems are also more capable of avoiding violations, leading to a positive correlation between CWS SDWA violations and upstream coal mining. We cannot control for the CWS characteristics that influence both where a mine is sited and how capable a CWS is at managing violations, which leads to omitted variable bias in an OLS estimate of the impact of coal mines on CWS drinking water quality violations. The example of politically capable CWSs being able to avoid violations and prevent upstream coal mining from taking place would create a positive bias on the coefficient of the number of mines in a regression on CWS violations since an increase in the number of mines is associated with less capable CWSs that have more violations.

\subsection{Instrument validity}

The instrument is independent if the relationship between CWS characteristics important for water quality and the siting of upstream coal mines is independent of the upstream sulfur coal sulfur content in 1995, so that $f(UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t, \text{CWS characteristic})=f(UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t)f(\text{CWS characteristic})$. Conditional on the control variables, $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%\times Post95$ is independent of the omitted variables in $\epsilon_{ct}$ that determine CWS drinking water quality violations. The ARP and the timing of the first phase were both exogenous to CWS drinking water quality and CWS characteristics. The ARP was a national clean air policy that was not written or enacted to address water quality issues. The effects of the ARP on coal production were determined by national and international energy markets that local CWSs or local CWS water quality did not affect. Local CWS characteristics regarding water quality or local power over source protection did not affect the scope of the ARP, nor how stringent the ARP law was, nor the degree to which low sulfur coal should be part of the abatement technology, nor the date of phase I of the ARP. The timing of the ARP did not coincide with major drinking water regulations. 

The instrument would violate independence if the unobserved CWS characteristics that affect drinking water quality violations and the siting of coal mines changed after 1995 for CWS with different amounts of sulfur as a percentage of coal weight. Knowing about sulfur as a percentage of coal weight upstream of CWS after 1995 tells us nothing about how much political power a local PWS has over mine siting. Thus, $Cov(\text{political power of CWS}_c, UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t)=0$. 

The instrument only affects CWSs through its effect on the number of coal mines. A threat to this would be if CWS SDWA regulation changed after 1995 and had a differential impact on CWSs downstream of high sulfur coal before and after 1995 compared to CWSs downstream of low sulfur coal before and after 1995. Changes in SDWA rules that affected all CWSs the same way are controlled for with time fixed effects. For example, in 1991, the monitoring and reporting schedule changed for all CWSs and is controlled for with time fixed effects. Any change to an MCL would apply to all CWSs and be controlled for with time fixed effects. The SDWA amendments that were passed in 1996 increased the use of consumer confidence reports as an enforcement tool when a CWS violated a drinking water regulation. CWSs downstream of high sulfur coal will experience less upstream coal mining after 1995 compared to before 1995, and therefore less upstream water contamination, whereas CWSs downstream of low sulfur coal will experience more upstream coal mining after 1995 compared to before 1995. CWSs downstream of high sulfur coal may experience fewer SDWA violations from upstream pollution compared to before 1995, meaning the burden of a consumer confidence report is smaller than it would have been before 1995. Whereas CWSs downstream of low sulfur coal will experience more upstream pollution after 1995 compared to before, meaning the burden of the consumer confidence report is higher than it would have been before 1995. This difference is likely small since all CWS in the sample are downstream of coal mining and have more similar pollution exposure and burden from consumer confidence reports compared to the average CWS in the country.

We can check this by looking at the relationships between ARPxpost95 and cws characteristics not associated with the number of upstream mines.

The instrument does predict the location and size of coal production. Figure \ref{fig:scatterhuccoalsulfur} is a scatter plot of the number of coal mines and the coal sulfur percent in the HUC12's upstream of CWSs before and after 1995, with plotted linear lines of best fit for each period. The change in production away from high sulfur coal production after the ARP is strongly demonstrated by the line of best fit flattening after 1995, indicating that while there was more coal production in higher sulfur coal areas before 1995, there was a large shift to areas with low sulfur coal after 1995. The number of coal mines in a HUC12 increased with the percent of sulfur as the weight of coal in the HUC12 before 1995. The time contingent switch in coal production based on sulfur content supports the structure of the variable $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t$. 

The strength of the instrument is still evident after controlling for covariates. Figure \ref{fig:first_stage_scatter_dwnstrm} is a scatter plot of the residualized number of upstream coal mines and the residualized instrumental variable. There is a negative relationship between the residualized number of upstream coal mines and $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \cdot Post95_t$.

\subsubsection{Assessing monotonicity}

We need the instrument to exhibit monotonicity and for there to be no defiers to recover the local average treatment effect (LATE) estimate of the instrument. A defier is a CWS that is upstream of a high-sulfur coal watershed where coal production increased after 1995 but would have decreased production after 1995 if it had instead been a low-sulfur coal watershed. A never-taker is a high-HUC12 that maintains production after 1995 because its customers are electricity generators that install scrubbers. As long as scrubber adoption is not positively correlated with sulfur in a way that causes production to increase post-ARP in high-sulfur areas, this is not a violation of monotonicity and only reduces first-stage power. To be a defier with scrubber installation requires a power plant to expand production after installing scrubbers and increase electricity market share. Power plants that rely on high sulfur coal and scrubbers are more likely to lose market share and reduce production since scrubbers are expensive to install, increase operating costs, and require higher electricity rates to retain profits. If some high-sulfur regions had a comparative advantage in low-cost production that offset the ARP cost increase, such as very low labor costs or transportation advantages, they might expand production even as ARP made their coal relatively more expensive. Unless this expansion would not have occurred if the low cost high sulfur regions had been low sulfur coal regions, this would not create defiers and would instead create never-takers.

Stating monotonicity more formally and following \cite{angrist1994identification}, let $\Psi$ be the support of the instrument $Z_i$, and for each $z \in \Psi$ define a random variable $D_i(z)$. The monotonicity assumption requires that for all $z, w \in \Psi$, either $D_i(z) \leq D_i(w)$ for all $i$, or $D_i(z) \geq D_i(w)$ for all $i$. This condition applies pairwise to elements of $\Psi$ and imposes no restriction on the ordering of $z$ and $w$. In this setting, the instrument is $Z_i = \text{post95}_t \times \text{sulfur}_h$, which equals zero before 1995 and $\text{sulfur}_h$ after. For a given sulfur level $w$, monotonicity requires either $\text{num_mines}_i(0) \leq \text{num_mines}_i(w)$ for all $i$, or $\text{num_mines}_i(0) \geq \text{num_mines}_i(w)$ for all $i$. This permits the direction of the response to vary with sulfur content. For example, at $\text{sulfur} = 1$ (low sulfur), mines may expand after 1995, so monotonicity requires $\text{num_mines}_i(0) \leq \text{num_mines}_i(1)$ for all $i$. At $\text{sulfur} = 4$ (high sulfur), mines likely contract after 1995, so monotonicity requires $\text{num_mines}_i(0) \geq \text{num_mines}_i(4)$ for all $i$. These two conditions are compatible: monotonicity is satisfied so long as each pairwise comparison is uniform across units, even when low- and high-sulfur areas respond in opposite directions.

\subsection{Results}

Table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_minevio_allcat} reports the results of equation \ref{2ndstage2sls} estimating the impact of the number of active upstream coal mines on the number of days in a year that a CWS spends in either an MCL or MR violation for nitrates, arsenic, and inorganic chemicals using OLS, reduced form, and two stage least squares regressions. 

An additional active coal mine upstream increases nitrate, arsenic, and IOC SDWA violations by roughly three days out of the year when we use OLS. The effect is statistically significant at the ten and five percent levels, respectively.

Coal production fell most acutely in high sulfur coal areas after 1995, and a positive relationship between coal production and SDWA violations predicts that IOC violations would fall with increasing upstream sulfur after 1995 relative to before 1995. The reduced form effect of the instrument measures how much more (or less) a one-percentage-point increase in upstream sulfur affects violations after ARP Phase I relative to before. An additional percent of coal sulfur upstream leads to statistically significantly fewer days a CWS experiences a nitrate, arsenic, or inorganic chemical violation of any kind after 1995 compared to before 1995. This suggests that in areas where the number of coal mines is more likely to decrease, the number of days in violation falls.

The instrumental variable estimate of an additional active coal mine upstream on nitrates and arsenic violations is an additional 19.9 and 14.6 days out of the year with SDWA violations, respectively. The instrument has a relatively strong first stage with an F statistic of 27.5. The OLS results underestimate the effect of coal mines on violations relative to the IV results. In general, the OLS impact of coal mines is five times smaller than the IV coefficient, suggesting that the omitted variable bias is negative. A negative omitted variable bias is consistent with CWSs that are able to influence the siting of upstream mines are better at avoiding violations. 

The entire increase in violations from upstream mining is driven by MR violations rather than MCL violations. The regressions presented in table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_minevio_mr} estimate equation \ref{2ndstage2sls} on MR arsenic, nitrate, and IOC violations. An additional mine upstream of a CWS leads to 19.9, 14.5, and 14 more days of monitoring and reporting violations for nitrates, arsenic, and general IOC contaminants respectively, comprising the entire effect observed when the dependent variable combines MR violations with MCL violations. IOC violations are statistically significant at the 10\% level when violations are limited to MR violations and statistically insignificant when the violations refer to the share of the year spent in either MCL or MR violations.

The impact of coal mining on downstream CWS MCL violations for nitrates, arsenic, and general IOC contaminants is presented in table \ref{2sls_dwnstrm_minevio_mcl}. The IV regression of the effect of the number of coal mines upstream estimates statistically and economically insignificant changes in the amount of time a CWS spends in an arsenic and nitrate MCL violation. The effect is less than an additional day out of the year. An additional coal mine upstream is associated with two fewer days of maximum contaminant level violations for inorganic chemicals; however, the effect is not statistically significant at any level.

Upstream coal mining increases MR violations without increasing MCL violations. Despite all the significant effects of coal mining being concentrated in MR violations and not in MCL violations, we cannot conclude that coal mining does not impact actual contaminant levels. A maximum contaminant level violation is only issued by the regulator when they receive the results of a test that a CWS conducted. Consistently failing to conduct tests or report the results of tests and incurring an MR violation prevents an MCL from being issued, even if contaminant concentration legitimately exceeds the MCL. The SDWA ECHO violation dataset is, for that reason, unable to measure drinking water contamination when systems do not monitor and report tests.

In the CWS compliance framework presented in the theoretical model, SDWA violations are a form of CWS non-compliance with environmental regulation. The theoretical model predicts that non-compliance will increase when compliance costs rise and/or the penalty or enforcement for non-compliance falls. We test whether compliance costs rise or whether penalties and enforcement fall with upstream coal mining.

\section{The impact of coal mining on downstream CWS contamination}

\subsection{Empirical Framework}

We estimate the effect of coal mining on IOC water contaminant concentrations at CWSs. This tests whether CWSs violated the SDWA due to rising compliance costs. Upstream mining increases the costs of compliance by raising the level of pollution and the effort that needs to be expended to stay in compliance. As the costs of compliance rise, the cost savings and, therefore, the benefits of non-compliance rise too. We limit the estimation to arsenic, nitrates, barium, chromium, and selenium. The MCL for these IOC contaminants did not change between 1985 and 2005 and were the IOC contaminants that were kept after cleaning the SYR2 data.

We estimate the impact of coal mining on the mean concentration of contaminants within downstream CWSs using the equation

\begin{equation}\label{eq:huc02reg}
    \textit{Concen}_{cht}= \beta\cdot\sum_{\tau=1985}^t\text{upstream coal prod}_{c\tau}  + \rho_c + HUC02_h\cdot\tau_t + \epsilon_{cht}
\end{equation}

Where $\textit{Concen}_{cht}$ is the mean concentration of a contaminant measured at CWS c in HUC02 h in year t using SYR2 data from 1997 to 2005. $\sum_{\tau=1985}^t\text{upstream coal prod}_{c\tau}$ is the cumulative tons of coal mined in all HUC12's directly upstream of all CWS c intakes from 1985 until year t. $\beta$ is the effect of an additional 10,000 tons of coal mined upstream of CWS c. CWS fixed effects are included as $\rho_c$, and to control for annual average pollution levels within the HUC02 watershed, we include HUC02 interacted with time fixed effects $HUC02_h\cdot\tau_t$. Standard errors are clustered at the CWS level.

Equation \ref{eq:huc02reg} is a continuous differences-in-differences regression with no pure control observations. The parallel trends assumption requires that the pollution concentrations at CWSs that experience a large decline in coal production would have trended the same as the pollution concentrations at CWSs with a small decline.

An additional 10 million cumulative short tons of coal mined upstream since 1985 increase mean concentrations of IOC contaminants at CWSs. The effect is significant for all IOC contaminants except for nitrates, which we lack the power to identify at a statistically significant level.

Arsenic increases by 0.0023 mg/L after an additional 10 million short tons of coal mined upstream. This is economically significant, equivalent to almost a 100\% increase over the mean arsenic concentration of 0.0029 mg/L, and is statistically significant. An additional 10 million short tons of coal increases nitrate concentrations by 0.05 mg/L; however, the effect is not economically or statistically significant. The effect represents a 5\% increase in concentration levels relative to the mean contaminant concentration of 0.75 mg/L.

of mean concentrations). Mining raises CWS costs to completely remove contaminants. CWSs downstream of mines face costlier SDWA compliance. \section{Regulator Responses to CWS's impacted by coal mining}

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}

We have established that coal mining increases CWSs violations of the SDWA. Violations increase as contaminant concentrations rise, which we have also established occurs when mining increases. The theoretical model predicts that violations will rise when enforcement or penalties for violations fall. We estimate whether regulatory enforcement action is less costly as coal mining increases upstream of CWSs. 

Coal mine siting and production could be endogenous to regulator enforcement of SDWA at CWSs. For example, CWSs that are able to protect their water sources from upstream mining would, on average, be better able at reducing the cost of regulatory enforcement they would face from SDWA violations. To address the endogeneity of mine siting and regulator action at CWSs, the number of upstream coal mines is instrumented with $\text{UpstreamCoalSulfur\%}_c \cdot \text{Post95}_t$ as in equation \ref{1ststage2sls}. We estimate the effect of upstream coal mining on the likelihood of formal and informal enforcement using IV for upstream coal mining. Formal enforcement is more severe and costlier than informal enforcement. 

Part of any effect that we find of coal mining has regulator enforcement of SDWA at CWSs is the effect of water pollution from coal mining on regulator enforcement. Regulators are more likely responding to pollution caused by upstream coal mining rather than by upstream coal mining itself since CWS water quality is regulated by state and federal authorities, and mine safety is regulated by the federal MSHA. It is possible that state environmental regulators are aware of coal mining pollution through their primacy to enforce point source pollution in the Clean Water Act. Coal mines are point source polluters. Regulators may coordinate their response to the CWSs affected by coal mining with their knowledge of coal production itself.

The first stage is
\begin{equation}\label{1ststage2slsreg}
\begin{split}
\text{NumMines}_{ct} &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1\text{NumIntakes}_{ct} + \alpha_2\text{UpstreamCoalSulfur\%}_c \cdot \text{Post95}_t \\
&\quad + Y_t + C_c + \nu_{ct}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

And the second stage is
\begin{equation}\label{2ndstage2slsreg}
\begin{split}
\text{Regulatory Action}_{ct} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1\text{NumIntakes}_{ct} + \gamma\widehat{\text{NumMines}}_{ct} \\
&\quad + Y_t + C_c + \epsilon_{ct}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The Number of Mines that are one HUC12 upstream of a CWS c water intake in year t, $NumMines_{ct}$, are predicted by a function of the number of CWS intake facilities, $NumIntakes_{ct}$, the average coal sulfur percentage in upstream HUC12's where coal sulfur can be calculated, $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c$, interacted with a dummy indicator for the years after 1995, stage I of the ARP, $Post95_t$, year fixed effects, $Y_t$, CWS fixed effects, $C_c$, and an error term, $\nu_{ct}$. Standard errors are clustered at the CWS level.

The CWS fixed effects control for the effect that upstream sulfur is associated with a higher baseline of AMD which could increase pollution and therefore enforcement action outside of the effect of the instrument on coal mines.

The exclusion restriction fails when the instrument affects regulator behavior through another path outside of its effect on coal mining. The exclusion restriction would be threatened if the ARP Phase I directly changed state EPA enforcement practices in a way that affected CWSs differently according to the percent of sulfur by weight in upstream coal. However, states were not primarily responsible for enforcing the ARP; it was a federally regulated program that, by itself, would not have affected state primacy enforcement of the SDWA.

Any national regulatory changes to the SDWA that affected all CWSs are controlled for with time fixed effects. The threat to the exogeneity of the instrument would occur if regulatory enforcement in low sulfur regions before and after 1995 changed differently from the change in regulatory enforcement in high sulfur regions before and after 1995.

\subsection{Results}

Table \ref{tab:h3_inf_formal_d12} presents the coefficients from estimating equation \ref{2ndstage2slsreg} on the likelihood of formal and informal enforcement. Upstream coal mining leads to a reduction in formal enforcement and an effect close to zero for informal enforcement, although we lack the power to identify this effect with any statistical significance. Formal enforcement is relatively costlier than informal enforcement.

The OLS regression measures a 1.69 percentage point fall in any formal enforcement occurring at a CWS in the year when an additional mine operates upstream. The IV estimates an additional upstream coal mine leads to a 5.6 percentage point fall in the likelihood of a CWS receiving formal enforcement action. The omitted variable bias is positive and is consistent with the bias from omitting CWS ability to protect upstream water sources and influence SDWA regulatory enforcement. 

The coefficient of $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c \times Post95_t$ in the reduced form regression compares the enforcement at CWSs with higher sulfur levels after 1995 to those with lower sulfur levels. A one-percentage point increase in $UpstreamCoalSulfur\%_c$ is associated with a 1.45 percentage point increase in formal enforcement after 1995, compared to before 1995. Mining declines with sulfur after 1995, this means that places that likely experienced declines in mining after 1995 also experienced increases in formal enforcement compared to places that experienced mining increases. Formal enforcement is less likely to be used as upstream mining increases. 

The regulator necessarily lacks information about the quality of water at a CWS incurring an MR violation. \section{Conclusion}

Upstream coal mining induces lower CWS SDWA compliance in the form of higher rates of SDWA monitoring and reporting violations. CWS SDWA compliance becomes costlier as coal mining increases since an increase in coal mining leads to increases in inorganic chemical water contamination at CWSs which increases the cost of completely removing contaminants. We do not detect mean contaminant levels increasing above maximum contaminant levels and cannot conclude that upstream coal mining is increasing pollution to the extent that CWSs need to install improved abatement technology to meet SDWA maximum contaminant level compliance. CWS enforcement penalties are relatively less costly as coal mining increases. CWSs downstream of increasing coal mining receive less formal enforcement action, which is costlier compared to informal enforcement. Regulators increase sanitary visits at CWSs downstream of increasing coal mining. Given scarce resources, the regulator prioritizes sanitary visits to places with MR violations. The regulator can use sanitary visits to assess the threat posed to public health by an MR violation and can implement a less costly type of enforcement when the threat is discovered to be low. Coal mining induces an increase in CWS SDWA violations by increasing the cost of compliance and reducing the penalties for non-compliance.
